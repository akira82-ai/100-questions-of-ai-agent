# Chapter 1 · Getting Started Without the Friction

You have probably had one of those moments: you talked with AI all evening and ideas poured out, then the instant you closed the window it was as if none of it happened. The next day you try to retrieve it and only remember that yesterday you discussed something impressive; the specifics are gone.

Or this: your WeChat favorites hold eighty-plus "must-read-later" items, and you have not opened a single one in half a year. You did not forget them; you simply cannot recall that you ever saved them.

Or this: you switched to a new project and AI does not recognize what you wrote last quarter, so you have to explain the background from scratch again. You start to wonder whether your memory is deteriorating, or whether the tool was never able to remember you in the first place.

This is not on you. AI starts from blank every time; it does not know you. The more you rely on it, the more you need a place to catch yourself. That place is your Obsidian vault. Drop the conversation into your own vault, and AI can pick up your context instead of starting blank every time.

So what happens when you actually put that place to use? Here are three real pictures. One person poured reading highlights, journal fragments, goal lists, and a schedule into a single local vault; switching to any new project, AI answers his last-quarter context in seconds, so writing and decisions no longer begin from blank. Another receives a one-screen briefing every morning, weather, yesterday's progress, and to-do deadlines all there, and AI even pulls hidden patterns from his link network that he never noticed, like a certain type of goal always stalling at quarter-end. Someone else moved back from a paid cloud tool to local Obsidian just so AI could finally understand the inspiration they had accumulated for years; a voice memo in the car gets saved, a tweet or video drops into the vault with one click.

This book is for people who want their daily input to turn into accumulation and output, and for people willing to do a little manual tagging while they write. It is not for people chasing zero-manual full automation, not for people who treat AI as a replacement for their own thinking, and not for people who need an entire team to share one knowledge base. If you fall into the latter groups, the play styles ahead may disappoint you.

## 1. I chat with AI every day and forget it all. Did I waste my time? First puncture this claim, then act

A grad-school candidate spent three nights using AI to sort out wrong-answer notes, feeling enlightened while chatting. The night before the exam, trying to review, he scrolled through the chat history and only remembered being excited; exactly what he got wrong and how to fix it was gone. You chat with AI two hours a day, and the moment you close the window, that spark of insight just evaporates, because every new conversation the AI does not recognize you, it has no idea where you left off last week or where you got stuck. Storing without processing, the things piled in your favorites also slowly lose value, and before long you cannot even be bothered to open them. Plenty of people only realized after deleting their so-called second brain that what actually proved useful in the end was the few lines they wrote by hand.

The first step is not to rush into learning tools. Every time something useful comes up in a chat, spend thirty seconds dropping it into a fixed place. Even just three lines: what you were solving, the one key sentence the AI gave, what to try next. Give AI a place of your own, and it truly works for you, instead of making things up from nothing. Don't worry about whether it is tidy yet. Save it first.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
## [Date] Useful things from my AI chat
- Context: what I was solving
- Takeaway: the one key sentence the AI gave
- Next: what I will try next
```

Someone admitted their self-improvement favorites were never opened again afterward. Someone else accumulated years of inspiration on a paid cloud tool, but AI could not read its nested structure, until they moved the content to local Obsidian and for the first time let AI truly understand their whole accumulation. The knowledge management that truly helps people is about making what you recorded retrievable and reusable, not dropping it into a drawer never opened again.

Moving to Obsidian is different. Every note can connect with others and be searched. What you accumulated in WeChat, once imported, truly enters your knowledge flow, instead of gathering dust on someone else's turf. Today, pick one item from your favorites, rewrite it in your own words, and save it into your vault. That is the first time it is actually used by you.

## 3. Everyone says the smarter AI gets, the less you need notes. Where is the trap?

An operations person relied on AI to write weekly reviews; after switching business lines, AI did not recognize the pitfalls he had hit last quarter, and he had to re-explain the background, only then realizing he had never truly kept those lessons. You have surely heard it: AI knows everything, so someday you won't need to memorize or take notes, just hand it all over. Sounds great. But the people who actually believed it, looking back three months later, find one thing: they forgot everything they talked about.

Don't rush to blame your memory for getting worse; the gap is not you, it's AI. It starts blank every time, with no context of yours. The trap is treating AI as your memory. Every time it opens a new conversation it does not know you. It does not know what you were mulling over last week, where you got stuck, what you concluded. You close the window and those sparks evaporate. You take notes to leave clues for your future self. AI cannot see those. It starts from blank every time; the context you feed it only lives inside that one window. It will not proactively connect this chat with what you wrote before; cross-session continuity depends on you maintaining it. Taking notes was never for AI to read. It is so your future self can pick up the thread next time. Many people feel AI is useless, but really they just never fed it the right context at the right moment; feed the background accurately, and it goes from okay to game-changing.

## 4. Before letting AI help me remember, first decide: "remember for future me" or "remember for AI"?

A freelancer fed all their client preferences to AI; after switching to a new model those preferences were all lost, and the client said he was inconsistent, not remembering what was said. If you want AI to help you remember, first get one thing clear: do you write to leave clues for your future self, or directly for AI to read? Get this order reversed and everything after is a mess.

Someone only understood after angrily deleting their so-called second brain that what it ignored was your existing accumulation; it only saw the present moment you fed in. Others found even the smartest AI will not proactively connect this chat with what you wrote before. So the right order is write for a human first: put your idea down in your own words, tag a few labels, so your future self can pick up the thread.

Once you write clearly, AI has something to read and connect. Today pick one thing you most need to remember this week, write three lines in your own words, and do not just throw a pile of unlabeled raw material at AI, because what it produces is rootless fragments.

But the people who use it smoothly almost all started from a handful of scattered notes. There was a grad-school candidate who at first just dropped in the fragments from daily review, and later the vault was full of things that grew from few to many. Someone else reviewing three years of notes said their biggest relief was not waiting until ready to start writing.

Few notes is not a weakness; it is actually the best time to build the habit, because there is no historical baggage to sort. Start now, write down one idea that came up today. The AI era needs exactly this, accumulating from the present. Later when you connect AI, what it reads is the things you grew along the way, not a pile you stuffed in temporarily.

## 6. First time opening Obsidian, what are the only three things I should do? One more is a burden

Someone installed the software, stared at a blank page for two hours, and finally closed it. Online tutorials start with bidirectional links, plugins, MOCs, and the more you read the more panicked you get. Beginners most easily fall into over-configuring, wanting to build a perfect system before writing a single word. Some joked they fell into a configuration rabbit hole, installed a pile of plugins and themes, yet wrote few notes.

The core of Obsidian is a local Markdown folder, not those fancy tricks. In your first week you only need three things: create a vault of your own, keep an inbox for temporary fragments, and write your first daily note to get the feel back. Plugins, links, graph, all set aside. Talk about optimization only after you are writing into it every day. Too many plugins and the vault gets slow, which makes it even easier to quit.

Open the app, create a vault, do these three steps:

1. Create folder `00-Inbox`, where all temporary material goes from now on.
2. Create folder `01-Diary`, one note per day, named `YYYY-MM-DD`.
3. Write today's diary, even if just three lines.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
my-vault/
├── 00-Inbox/    # temporary material, to be sorted
├── 01-Diary/    # named by date
├── 03-Projects/ # things in progress
├── 05-Resources/ # reading / knowledge cards
└── 99-Attachments/ # images, PDFs
```

You have seen those perfect knowledge-base screenshots, folders neat, links dense, and one glance makes you feel far behind. This anxiety is common, but it is an illusion. Someone migrating from another note app made throwing away the pursuit of perfection their first move, keeping only what they actually used.

## 8. Why is local Markdown so good, and why am I not afraid of the software dying?

Someone was using a note app that suddenly announced it was shutting down; five years of notes exported as a mess of garbled text, and only then did he realize he had never truly owned those words. Some worry: what if Obsidian stops someday, aren't my years of notes locked in? This worry basically does not hold up against local Markdown.

Your notes are a bunch of plain-text files on your own disk, not dependent on any software's private format. Switch software someday, even no software, any notepad can open them. Someone migrating from another tool valued exactly this: the files always belong to you, leave whenever you want.

One bottom line: the software is the shell, your files are the asset. So you are not afraid of it dying, because it never truly owned your notes, it just helped you write plain text a little more comfortably.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
# H1 title  ## H2  ### H3
**bold** *italic*
- unordered  /  1. ordered
- [ ] todo  - [x] done
> quote
[[internal link]]  [[internal link|display text]]
```

## 9. How do I sync notes between phone and computer with the least fuss? Don't jump to plugins

Someone wanted to read a bit they had written that morning on the subway, only to find it had not synced, a letdown for nothing. If notes do not sync between phone and computer, they break in half, never at hand when you need them. Someone to save a few dollars a month used iCloud or Dropbox for free sync, only to hit conflicting files and 0kb ghost files on the phone, even overwriting existing content, ending up either losing notes or finally buying official Sync. Someone tried a dozen sync solutions on the market one by one and found the two least fussy were actually the simplest.

One is Obsidian's official Sync, designed specifically for a local vault like yours, auto-aligning phone and computer, with the downside of a paid subscription. The other is even simpler: drop your vault folder straight into a system cloud drive and let it sync in the background, no plugins at all.

Don't touch Git, don't touch third-party sync plugins; those are for people with complex multi-device needs. As long as your vault stays in one folder inside the cloud drive, open it on another device and it is the same copy. When the day comes that the official Sync subscription hurts your wallet, consider something else then. It is not too late.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
# Sync options (newbies pick the least fussy first)
- Official Sync: phone and computer auto-align, paid subscription, most worry-free
- System cloud drive: drop vault folder into iCloud / OneDrive / Nutstore, background sync, zero plugins
- Obsidian Git: private GitHub repo + version rollback, free but needs some setup
Suggestion: start with the cloud-drive folder, don't touch Git or third-party sync plugins up front
```

## 10. Folders or bidirectional links? Give me a non-debating rule

Beginners most easily get stuck choosing between folders and links, debating for ages. Actually you do not have to choose; they handle two different things.

Stable things with clear boundaries go in folders. Like your projects, your learning areas, things that will not belong to A today and B tomorrow. Put them in folders and it is obvious at a glance. Things that cross over repeatedly, connectable from different angles, use links. Like a concept that relates both to this reading note and to that project. Use `[[ ]]` to string it together, and later you can jump from any note.

Someone migrating from another app made dropping the pursuit of a perfect folder tree their first move, switching to MOCs to gather same-topic notes. The rule in one sentence: things you can categorize go in folders, things that cross over get linked, and the two combined feel smooth.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
# Folders vs links rule
- Stable, clear-boundary (projects / learning areas) → folder
- Repeatedly crossing over, multi-angle (a concept) → [[link]]
- Combine the two: folders as skeleton, links as relationships, MOC gathers same topic
```

## 12. Is the free version of Obsidian enough, or should I buy membership? Clear the boundary

Many people get uneasy at the word membership, afraid functionality is locked without paying. For personal local use, Obsidian is completely free. All core editing, linking, and plugin abilities are yours, no feature-paywall.

The paid part is called Obsidian Sync and Publish. Let me be clear about the boundary: Sync is the official cross-device sync service, Publish sends your vault to the web for others to see. These two are extra services, not locks on the software itself. Use a system cloud drive to sync, use local files, not a cent spent. Some also found that however fancy the desktop setup, many plugins are unsupported on mobile and open slowly, and the cross-device experience falls short of expectations, true for both free and paid, so know it going in.

So the rule is simple: as long as you write on your own computer and sync with a cloud drive or official Sync, free is enough. Only if you want officially hosted sync, or want to publish notes as a public site, do you need to consider paying.

## 13. Can I hand privacy-sensitive content (diary / client) to cloud AI right away?

Diary, client materials, this kind of sensitive content handed to cloud AI right away carries a bigger risk than you imagine. Someone for convenience dropped their diary straight to a cloud large model, only later realizing that most private content had already left the machine. Someone specifically took apart the attack surface, saying the vulnerability is not just the content itself, but untrusted content stacked with the outbound channel.

The official stance is also clear: fewer plugins means a smaller attack surface. So for this kind of content, do not rush to connect a cloud model. Two steadier paths: either use a local model, data never leaves your computer; or encrypt before storing, say wrap the vault with Cryptomator, then decide whether to connect any external service.

One bottom line: protect private content first, then talk about intelligence. Once you are clear about which data should or should not leave local, then touch AI with peace of mind.

## 14. Minimum viable path: how to experience "AI reads my notes" once with minimal config?

Someone connected their vault as instructed, asked it what they had been wrestling with last week, and it really pulled the answer from their diary. That was the moment they understood what an AI that gets me means. You want to experience AI reading your notes, but fear the config is too troublesome. Actually the minimum config is just two steps, no need to understand the principle first.

Install one Copilot plugin, the one in Obsidian that chats with AI directly and can also answer based on your notes. Then give it a model source: either apply for a chat-model key and paste it in, or run an open-source model locally, data never leaves the machine.

Once installed, open the diary you wrote today and ask it which to-dos I mentioned in this note. When it actually pulls the answer from your words, you have felt the hand of an AI that gets me. If installing a plugin feels like too much, there is an even lighter path: paste the note-file path straight to a desktop AI, and it can locate and rewrite the corresponding file, so you taste AI reading your notes with zero plugins.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
Copilot three settings (follow the plugin's latest README):
- Base URL: fill to the model provider address, must end with /v1
- Model: fill the model name, copy exactly
- API Key: shown only once, store in a password manager, never into git
```
