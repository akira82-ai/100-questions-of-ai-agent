# Chapter 1 · Getting Started Without the Friction

You have probably had one of those moments: you talked with AI all evening and ideas poured out, then the instant you closed the window it was as if none of it happened. The next day you try to retrieve it and only remember that yesterday you discussed something impressive; the specifics are gone.

Or this: your WeChat favorites hold eighty-plus must-read-later items, and you have not opened a single one in half a year. You did not forget them; you simply cannot recall that you ever saved them.

Or this: you switched to a new project and AI does not recognize what you wrote last quarter, so you have to explain the background from scratch again. You start to wonder whether your memory is deteriorating, or whether the tool was never able to remember you in the first place.

This is not on you. AI starts from blank every time; it does not know you. The more you rely on it, the more you need a place to catch yourself. That place is your Obsidian vault. Drop the conversation into your own vault, and AI can pick up your context instead of starting blank every time.

So what happens when you actually put that place to use? Here are three real pictures. Pour reading highlights, journal fragments, goal lists, and a schedule into a single local vault, and switching to any new project AI answers your last-quarter context in seconds; receive a one-screen briefing every morning with weather, yesterday's progress, and to-do deadlines all there, and AI even pulls hidden patterns from your link network that you never noticed; move back from a paid cloud tool to local Obsidian so AI can finally understand the inspiration you accumulated for years, with a voice memo in the car saved and a tweet or video dropped straight into the vault.

This book is for people who want their daily input to turn into accumulation and output, and for people willing to do a little manual tagging while they write. It is not for people chasing zero-manual full automation, not for people who treat AI as a replacement for their own thinking, and not for people who need an entire team to share one knowledge base. If you fall into the latter groups, the play styles ahead may disappoint you.

## 1. I chat with AI every day and forget it all. Did I waste my time? First puncture this claim, then act

Verdict upfront: you did not waste your time. Chatting without saving, though, lends your ideas to a tool that cannot remember you. A grad-school candidate spent three nights using AI to sort out wrong-answer notes, feeling enlightened while chatting. The night before the exam, trying to review, he scrolled through the chat history and only remembered being excited; exactly what he got wrong and how to fix it was gone. You chat with AI two hours a day, and the moment you close the window that spark of insight just evaporates, because every new conversation the AI does not recognize you, it has no idea where you left off last week or where you got stuck. Storing without processing, the things piled in your favorites also slowly lose value, and before long you cannot even be bothered to open them. The real fix is not to chat less, it is to drop a note the moment you finish.

Step for today: every time something useful comes up in a chat, spend thirty seconds dropping it into a fixed place. Even just three lines: what you were solving, the one key sentence the AI gave, what to try next. Give AI a place of your own, and it truly works for you, it will not make things up from nothing. Don't worry about whether it is tidy yet. Save it first.

Resource: Obsidian Help https://help.obsidian.md/

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
## [Date] Useful things from my AI chat
- Context: what I was solving
- Takeaway: the one key sentence the AI gave
- Next: what I will try next
```

## 2. I used WeChat favorites as my second brain, why can I never remember to use it?

Verdict: favoriting is burying. It is not your knowledge flow, it is a pile of islands on someone else's turf. Someone accumulated over three hundred WeChat favorites, each tagged read later, and opened fewer than five on their own in a whole year. The problem is those items lie inside WeChat, an island, not connected to your other notes and not uniformly searchable. Once you move the content to local Obsidian, AI can finally read your whole accumulation, because the files belong to you and the structure is transparent. The knowledge management that truly helps is about making what you recorded retrievable and reusable, not dropping it into a drawer never opened again.

Step for today: pick one item from your favorites, rewrite it in your own words, and save it into your vault. That is the first time it is actually used by you. In Obsidian every note can connect with others and be searched, so what you accumulated in WeChat truly enters your knowledge flow only after you import it.

## 3. Everyone says the smarter AI gets, the less you need notes. Where is the trap?

Verdict: the trap is treating AI as your memory. An operations person relied on AI to write weekly reviews; after switching business lines, AI did not recognize the pitfalls he had hit last quarter, and he had to re-explain the background, only then realizing he had never truly kept those lessons. You take notes to leave clues for your future self. AI cannot see those, it does not know you every time it opens a new conversation. It will not proactively connect this chat with what you wrote before; cross-session continuity depends on you maintaining it. So the point is who you write for: write for a human first, then let AI read what you wrote.

Step for today: after today's AI chat, pick one conclusion you most agree with, write it into your vault in three lines, with the context, the takeaway, and the next step. Feed the background accurately, and AI goes from okay to genuinely useful.

## 4. Before letting AI help me remember, first decide: "remember for future me" or "remember for AI"?

Verdict: the right order is write for a human first, then let AI read. A freelancer fed all their client preferences to AI; after switching to a new model those preferences were all lost, and the client said he was inconsistent, not remembering what was said. That so-called second brain ignores your existing accumulation; it only sees the present moment you fed in. Even the smartest AI will not proactively connect this chat with what you wrote before. So put your idea down in your own words first, tag a few labels, so your future self can pick up the thread.

Step for today: pick one thing you most need to remember this week, write three lines in your own words, and do not just throw a pile of unlabeled raw material at AI, because what it produces is rootless fragments. Once you write clearly, AI has something to read and connect.

The people who use it smoothly almost all started from a handful of scattered notes. There was a grad-school candidate who at first just dropped in the fragments from daily review, and later the vault was full of things that grew from few to many.

Step for today: write down one idea that came up today, even if just three lines. The AI era needs exactly this, accumulating from the present. Later when you connect AI, what it reads is the things you grew along the way, not a pile you stuffed in temporarily.

## 6. First time opening Obsidian, what are the only three things I should do? One more is a burden

Verdict: in your first week only three things matter, set plugins, links, and graph aside. Someone installed the software, stared at a blank page for two hours, and finally closed it. Online tutorials start with bidirectional links, plugins, MOCs, and the more you read the more panicked you get. Beginners most easily fall into over-configuring, wanting to build a perfect system before writing a single word. Too many plugins and the vault gets slow, which makes it even easier to quit.

The core of Obsidian is a local Markdown folder, not those fancy tricks. In your first week you only need three things: create a vault of your own, keep an inbox for temporary fragments, and write your first daily note to get the feel back. Talk about optimization only after you are writing into it every day.

Open the app, create a vault, do these three steps:

1. Create folder `00-Inbox`, where all temporary material goes from now on.
2. Create folder `01-Diary`, one note per day, named `YYYY-MM-DD`.
3. Write today's diary, even if just three lines.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
my-vault/
├── 00-Inbox/    # temporary material, to be sorted
├── 01-Diary/    # named by date
├── 03-Projects/ # things in progress
├── 05-Resources/ # reading / knowledge cards
└── 99-Attachments/ # images, PDFs
```

## 7. Seeing other people's perfect vault screenshots makes me more anxious. How do I become immune?

Verdict: those screenshots were curated before posting; they are not your passing line. Someone migrating from another note app made throwing away the pursuit of perfection their first move, keeping only what they actually used. You did not see the chaos they deleted behind them, the half-finished attempts they abandoned. One handy vault beats ten pretty ones, and behind those pretty vaults there is often a large stretch never actually read.

The cure is simple: other people's structure is for reference, not for copying. First figure out what you want to record, then start with the smallest one or two folders. See a pretty screenshot, like it and scroll past. Do not use it as your standard.

Step for today: next time you see a perfect vault screenshot, like it and scroll past, and today just create one `00-Inbox` folder and stop there.

## 8. Why is local Markdown so good, and why am I not afraid of the software dying?

Verdict: the software is the shell, your files are the asset. Someone was using a note app that suddenly announced it was shutting down; five years of notes exported as a mess of garbled text, and only then did he realize he had never truly owned those words. Your notes are a bunch of plain-text files on your own disk, not dependent on any software's private format. Switch software someday, even no software, any notepad can open them. The files always belong to you, leave whenever you want.

One bottom line: you are not afraid of it dying, because it never truly owned your notes, it just helped you write plain text a little more comfortably.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
# H1 title  ## H2  ### H3
**bold** *italic*
- unordered  /  1. ordered
- [ ] todo  - [x] done
> quote
[[internal link]]  [[internal link|display text]]
```

## 9. How do I sync notes between phone and computer with the least fuss? Don't jump to plugins

Verdict: for newbies there are only two least-fussy paths, so don't touch Git or third-party sync plugins yet. Someone wanted to read a bit they had written that morning on the subway, only to find it had not synced, a letdown for nothing. If notes do not sync between phone and computer, they break in half, never at hand when you need them. To save a few dollars a month using iCloud or Dropbox for free sync, you easily hit conflicting files and 0kb ghost files on the phone, even overwriting existing content, and end up buying official Sync anyway.

One is Obsidian's official Sync, designed specifically for a local vault like yours, auto-aligning phone and computer, with the downside of a paid subscription. The other is even simpler: drop your vault folder straight into a system cloud drive and let it sync in the background, no plugins at all.

Step for today: move your vault folder into iCloud / OneDrive / Nutstore and let the system cloud drive sync it in the background, zero plugins. When the day comes that the official Sync subscription hurts your wallet, consider something else then. It is not too late.

Resource: Obsidian Sync https://obsidian.md/sync

## 10. Folders or bidirectional links? Give me a non-debating rule

Verdict: you do not have to choose; they handle two different things. Someone spent an afternoon agonizing over how to categorize notes and wrote not a single word. Stable things with clear boundaries go in folders, like projects and learning areas, obvious at a glance. Things that cross over repeatedly, connectable from different angles, use links, like a concept that relates both to this reading note and to that project. Use `[[ ]]` to string it together, and later you can jump from any note.

My advice is to drop the pursuit of a perfect folder tree and use MOCs to gather same-topic notes, which is where most people landing from other apps end up. The rule in one sentence: things you can categorize go in folders, things that cross over get linked, and the two combined feel smooth.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
# Folders vs links rule
- Stable, clear-boundary (projects / learning areas) → folder
- Repeatedly crossing over, multi-angle (a concept) → [[link]]
- Combine the two: folders as skeleton, links as relationships, MOC gathers same topic
```

Resource: Obsidian Help https://help.obsidian.md/

## 12. Is the free version of Obsidian enough, or should I buy membership? Clear the boundary

Verdict: for personal local use, Obsidian is completely free. All core editing, linking, and plugin abilities are yours, no feature-paywall. Someone gets uneasy at the word membership, afraid functionality is locked without paying, and never dares to start. The paid part is called Obsidian Sync and Publish: Sync is the official cross-device sync service, Publish sends your vault to the web for others to see. These two are extra services, not locks on the software itself. Use a system cloud drive to sync, use local files, not a cent spent.

Step for today: as long as you write on your own computer and sync with a cloud drive or official Sync, free is enough. Only if you want officially hosted sync, or want to publish notes as a public site, do you need to consider paying. However fancy the desktop setup, many plugins are unsupported on mobile and open slowly, true for both free and paid, so know it going in.

Resource: Obsidian Sync https://obsidian.md/sync · Obsidian Publish https://obsidian.md/publish

## 13. Can I hand privacy-sensitive content (diary / client) to cloud AI right away?

Verdict: do not hand it over right away; protect private content first, then talk about intelligence. Someone for convenience dropped their diary straight to a cloud large model, only later realizing that most private content had already left the machine. Attack-surface analysis shows the vulnerability is the untrusted content stacked with the outbound channel, not only the content itself. The official stance is also clear: fewer plugins means a smaller attack surface.

Two steadier paths: either use a local model, data never leaves your computer; or encrypt before storing, say wrap the vault with Cryptomator, then decide whether to connect any external service.

Step for today: put diary and client materials into a separate local vault, and do not connect any cloud model yet. Once you are clear about which data should or should not leave local, then touch AI with peace of mind.

Resource: Cryptomator https://cryptomator.org/

## 14. Minimum viable path: how to experience "AI reads my notes" once with minimal config?

Verdict: the minimum config is just two steps, no need to understand the principle first. Someone connected their vault as instructed, asked it what they had been wrestling with last week, and it really pulled the answer from their diary. That was the moment they understood what an AI that gets me means. Install one Copilot plugin, the one in Obsidian that chats with AI directly and can also answer based on your notes. Then give it a model source: either apply for a chat-model key and paste it in, or run an open-source model locally, data never leaves the machine.

Step for today: open the diary you wrote today and ask it which to-dos I mentioned in this note. When it actually pulls the answer from your words, you have felt the hand of an AI that gets me. If installing a plugin feels like too much, there is an even lighter path: paste the note-file path straight to a desktop AI, and it can locate and rewrite the corresponding file, so you taste AI reading your notes with zero plugins.

Resource: Copilot https://community.obsidian.md/plugins/copilot
