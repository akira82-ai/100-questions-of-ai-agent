# Chapter 1 · Get Started Without Friction: Minimum Viable Obsidian + AI

Have you ever had one of those nights where you talk to AI for hours, ideas pouring out, and the moment you close the window it is as if nothing happened? The next day you want to find it back and all you remember is that you talked about something good, with zero detail left.

Or this: your WeChat favorites hold eighty plus articles saved for later, none opened in half a year. You did not forget, you simply cannot recall you ever saved them.

Or this: you switch to a new project and AI has no memory of what you wrote last quarter, so you explain the whole background from scratch again. You start to wonder whether it is your bad memory or the tool that never remembers you.

This is not your fault. AI starts blank every time and does not know you. The more you rely on it, the more you need a place that catches you. That place is your Obsidian vault. Drop your conversations into your own vault and AI can pick up your context instead of starting from zero each time.

What happens when you actually use this place? Three real pictures. You gather reading highlights, diary fragments, goal lists, and schedules into one local vault, and on any new project AI recalls last quarter's context in seconds, so writing and decisions no longer start blank. Each morning you get a one screen briefing with weather, yesterday's progress, and deadlines, and AI pulls hidden patterns from your link network, like a type of goal that always stalls at quarter end. You move back from a paid cloud tool to local Obsidian and let AI read the inspiration you accumulated for years, capturing voice memos the moment you get in the car and dropping tweets and videos straight into the vault.

This book is for people who want daily input to become accumulation and output, and for people willing to add a little manual tagging while taking notes. It is not for people chasing zero manual, fully automatic setups, not for people who use AI to think for them, and not for teams that need one shared knowledge base. If you are in the last groups, the path ahead may disappoint you.

## 1. I chat with AI every day and forget it all. Did I waste the chat? Pop that claim first, then act.

No, you did not waste it, but chatting without writing it down is basically the same as wasting it. Your most valuable thinking evaporates into the chat log and you end up with a pile that only takes in and never gives out.

Make yourself one hard rule. Every Q and A gives you two things: the answer itself, plus one note update dropped into your notes, even if it is just two lines appended to some page. Write this in stone, no choosing one or the other. Leave any slack and the loop stops immediately. Compounding comes from the loop, and the loop depends on forcing it. Relying on willpower does not survive two weeks.

Where you drop it does not need agonizing. Just split two layers. Raw chat logs, clippings, and articles go into a place that only adds and never reorganizes. It is not responsible for looking pretty, only for keeping original evidence. Conclusions, comparisons, and reports from the Q and A go somewhere else, that is your workbench. Ideas that pop up on the fly can sit in your phone memo first, and only the ones worth keeping move into the vault. Capture and storage each do their own job, do not expect one place to do both.

One more warning up front, do not spend weeks building structure on day one. A beautiful system with no change in your days is just procrastination dressed as productivity, and in the end you get a very tidy graveyard. Let things in first, structure grows later on its own.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
At the vault root create two folders:

raw/        raw chats, clippings, articles, add only, do not reorganize
outputs/    conclusions, comparisons, reports from Q and A

Then write this rule into your AI rules file (CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md):

Every time you answer, give two things:
1. the answer itself
2. one note update, even if just two lines appended to a related page
Never choose one or the other.
```

## 2. I use WeChat favorites as my second brain. Why can I never remember to use it?

Because saving it is basically burying it. When you save you think you will read it later, and later never happens. When you actually need it and go digging, half an hour of searching may still get you nothing, because that place cannot be searched, cannot be linked, and is not even yours.

You should move things, but do not move everything. Pick the content you will keep looking up, keep adding to, and keep using, and let go of the stuff you saved only to feel safe, since writing it down just relocates the hoarding. Old vault migration must accept the same math: an eighty percent success counts as a win. Run new stuff through the new structure first and digest the old slowly. A one time big cleanup drains you before it finishes.

Keep the cost of those manual steps low. Copy, strip formatting, write a title, that is several minutes, and when busy you skip it, and after skipping a few times the habit dies. Use Importer for batches, and for scattered items add one sentence on the source and why you kept it. That one sentence decides whether you and AI can pull it back later.

What makes a vault truly alive is the moment of use. Every time you pull up an old note, add a bit of new info. That beats hoarding it untouched, and beats the day you swear to reorganize everything from scratch.

## 3. Everyone says the stronger AI gets, the less you need notes. Where is the trap?

The trap is that AI has no stance, no experience, and no taste. If you feed it nothing, what it returns is always the generic, average version anyone could write. Same model, but feed in your own material, methods, experience, and judgment, and the output carries your mark.

So note taking was not killed by AI, it just changed identity, from archive to raw material. When taking notes follow one rule: assume the you of three months later has completely forgotten the context, and fill in the key details, background, and logic. This note is written at once for the future you and for the AI that reads your vault. Keep the format stable, the paths clear, and the content out of dozens of scattered chat windows, so it can pick up next time.

The trap on the other side is sneakier. Outsource the whole remembering job and you outsource reflection with it. You stop re examining ideas, stop questioning them, trust the structure once archived, and then nothing. Learning happens at the moment an external action triggers internal processing, and connecting new info to existing knowledge is exactly that kind of action. Letting AI do it skips the skill building itself. Do not outsource understanding, this line is hard here.

One step to land it: after a chat, pick the conclusion you agree with most and write it into the vault in your own words, one line each for background, conclusion, and next step. Get the background right and it goes from okay to actually helpful.

## 4. I want AI to remember for me. First decide: notes for future me, or for AI?

Write for the human first. The version written for a human is also readable by AI, but the reverse does not hold. Copy pasting raw is the same as zero. Rephrase it in your own words and that note starts to have value.

A note worth reusing later should not be only the copied original. Four blocks are enough: what this is, summarized in your own words; my judgment, why it is worth keeping; next step, what you plan to do with it; related, links to which projects or topics. Write one double link in related, and later you can click from this note into the topic page, and the topic page's backlinks will show this note again.

For the machine readable layer, just add a few fields. Title it with date plus scene plus topic, not vague names like new note or essay. The first sentence states plainly what problem this note solves, and AI uses it to judge relevance. Keep the source link, author, and time as they are for later tracing. Do not pile on fields, too many and nobody maintains them. Fix updated, status, summary, and source first, the ones you will actually use, then add as needed.

Do not reverse the order. You drop the thinking first, then AI has something to read and link. Hand it a pile of raw material with no annotations from you and what it produces is rootless fragments.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
---
created: 2026-07-31
updated: 2026-07-31
status: active
summary: what problem this note mainly solves
source: original source or link
---

## What this is
summarize in your own words

## My judgment
why it is worth keeping

## Next step
what you plan to do with it

## Related
- [[some project or topic]]
```

## 5. I have very few notes. Is it too early to start Obsidian now?

Not at all. Starting from an empty vault, zero plugins and zero starter kit, is the better opening, because you carry no history to clear.

The first time you use it, it may feel less convenient than a memo app, and that is normal. It is not a tool that works the moment you download it, you have to put real content in according to your own situation. Notes grow slowly, links build slowly, and that star map relationship graph grows naturally. Ten notes a week may feel like nothing, but a month and three months later is when it pays off. Use it half a year and you get something nobody can copy away.

Do not chase perfect notes. One line is worth writing, it is for you, not a portfolio. Write first, organize later. Do not spend two hours designing folder categories up front, you learn how to organize after you have written enough. When the same kind of note keeps appearing, make a template. When the same manual action keeps repeating, look for a plugin. Plugins reduce confirmed repetitive work, they do not pre add maintenance cost to a habit that has not formed.

If you have old notes, do not rush to move them all. Copy the important ones over first, bring the rest when you actually need them. A one time explosion migration is the easiest way to burn yourself out.

## 6. First time opening Obsidian, what are the only three things I should do? One more is a burden.

Create a vault, keep an inbox, write your first diary entry. Plugins, double links, and the graph view all wait.

A vault is just an ordinary folder on your computer, name it anything, put it on the desktop or in documents, not deep in the C drive. For a Chinese interface go to Settings, the About section, and pick Simplified Chinese under Language, then restart. The inbox is a folder where all temporary material lands first, and point the new note location in settings to it, or notes scatter everywhere. The diary is a core plugin you just turn on, one entry a day, named YYYY-MM-DD.

Do not touch community plugins on day one. In core plugins, turn on diary, outline, bookmarks, tags, and templates first. After a week or two of use, when native features truly fail a need, then search for the matching plugin. More installed does not mean better used. Install twenty or thirty and try each shallowly, and you end up with slower startup, messier interface, and most never used. By the way, several thousand md files in your vault will not slow it down, plugins are what usually drag it.

Two shortcuts are worth memorizing the same day. Ctrl or Cmd plus P is the command palette, search any function there when you cannot find it. Ctrl or Cmd plus O is quick switcher, type a note name and open it instantly, far faster than clicking through folders with a mouse.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
My Vault/
├── 00-Inbox/   # temporary material, to be organized
├── 01-Diary/   # named YYYY-MM-DD
├── 03-Projects/   # things in progress
├── 05-Resources/   # reading / knowledge cards
└── 99-Attachments/   # images PDF
```

## 7. I see others' perfect vault screenshots and feel more anxious. How do I detox?

Copying another user's complex vault forces you to do something that was never yours to begin with.

Those power user setups, when you open them up, are mostly dozens of plugins plus a three page diary, with dozens of subpages below recording life's trivia. That is a recipe for burnout, not your passing line. Worse, many are selling you their usage. You watch and slowly reshape your purpose into theirs, instead of reshaping the tool to fit you. You only wanted a place to write a diary, follow one tutorial round, and look back to find yourself building a media library and a knowledge graph, with zero diary entries written.

Tutorials themselves are a trap. A good tutorial still triggers a new round of changes, and the changes may not stick. A while later you see another one and want to tear it all down and restart. Set your own rules, write them down, and make a style guide that is yours. Consistency folds hundreds of future decisions into one, like always using plural tags, and you never agonize over how to name a new tag. Change it anytime you want.

Add complexity only when you hit a concrete block that clearly stops you from using it, not as a rehearsal in advance. When your hands itch to try another user's method, open a one off dummy vault to test, do not use your main vault as the lab.

## 8. Why is local Markdown good, and why am I not afraid of the software dying?

Because your notes are just a bunch of .md plain text files on the disk, no database, no proprietary format. The day Obsidian the company disappears, Notepad still opens them.

The trap of cloud note tools is data kidnapping. Proprietary formats export either HTML or rich text with a load of styles, not standard Markdown, so you cannot even leave cleanly. Plain text flips it: to let what you write still open on a 2060s computer, the key is that it must open on a 1960s computer. Apps are perishable, files are what get to stay.

This gains another layer in the AI era. All AI tools read Markdown directly, no export, no conversion, no upload. That pile of md sits locally, give access and it reads, searches, and continues writing. You can swap models anytime, use Git for version control, or run a local model, and the data never leaves the machine.

One thing not to confuse: local files do not equal backed up. Disk dies, hand slips and deletes, gone all the same. Sync solves phone and computer seeing the same copy, backup solves whether you can recover a mistaken delete. Do the two separately, do not expect one to cover both.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
# H1  ## H2  ### H3
**bold** *italic*
- bullet  /  1. ordered
- [ ] todo  - [x] done
> quote
[[internal link]]  [[internal link|display text]]
```

## 9. What is the easiest way to sync notes between phone and computer? Do not jump to plugins.

Beginners start light, pick one of official Sync, iCloud, or Nutstore. Git and self hosted stuff wait until a real pain point stops you.

Official Sync is end to end encrypted, natively supported on all platforms including iOS, near real time, with built in version history and conflict resolution, and support troubleshoots for you when something breaks. One downside, it is expensive. Standard is 4 dollars a month for one vault at 1 GB, Plus is 8 dollars a month for ten vaults at 10 GB. To save, drop the vault straight into your system cloud drive's sync folder and let the cloud client move files between devices. Obsidian itself does not handle sync at all, zero plugins.

The cloud drive path has a few pits to know first. iCloud on Windows causes file duplication or corruption, do not touch it. For OneDrive, mark the vault folder as always keep on this device and turn off files on demand, or Obsidian thinks files are gone. All three clouds share a flaw: delay of a few to dozens of seconds, not real time, and the .obsidian directory scatters a load of small files where cloud clients often stall. Also never sync the same folder with two cloud drives at once, conflict is certain.

Two principles hold regardless of plan. Editing on multiple devices at once inevitably makes conflict files no plan can cure, so build the habit of finishing on one end before switching. No matter how good the sync, it is not backup. Deletions and corruption get faithfully copied to every device, so a separate backup is mandatory.

## 10. Folders or double links, which do I pick? Give me a no agonizing method.

You do not pick one. Folders tell you where something lives, links and backlinks tell you what it relates to. They manage two different things.

Keep folders shallow, one to two levels max. Do not spend hours designing the perfect taxonomy. Designing categories before you have written a few notes guarantees a crash. Do not force links just to make the graph look good either. Only connect two notes when a real relation exists, source, extension, same project, support, or rebuttal. Once same topic notes pile up, make one MOC index note to string them together, which beats a pile of nested subfolders.

Both extremes have people living in them, and living well. Some barely use folders, because many notes belong to several fields at once and they refuse to decide where each goes. Others are thorough folder people whose brains melt if a file is not filed, and they use zero links. You do not have to pick a side, pick what feels comfortable.

If you want one method, write for half a month first, see which need emerges naturally, then act. Mixing MOC, PARA, and Zettelkasten is completely fine.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
# Folders vs double links, a method
- where it lives -> folder (shallow, 1-2 levels max)
- what it relates to -> [[link]] (connect only on source / extension / same project / support or rebuttal)
- same topic notes pile up -> build one MOC index note to string them
- unsure -> drop in 00-Inbox, write half a month first
```

## 11. Without any AI plugin, is a pure Obsidian week useful?

Yes, and you should do exactly that. In the beginner phase use it bare for a week or two, plugins come later.

Obsidian is not a tool that works the moment you download it. You have to put real content in according to your own situation first. This week do one small thing: drop every idea and every point from conversations into the inbox. Ignore links, ignore categories, ignore looks. First make writing a habit. Used a week it is at most a searchable note tool, used a month it slowly becomes something you can refer to, used half a year it grows into the knowledge engine that is only yours.

The right plugin order: when the same kind of note keeps appearing, make a template; when the same manual action keeps repeating, find a plugin. Reverse it, install a pile first then hunt for uses, and you are purely making work for yourself. Install a dozen or twenty up front, startup slows, interface clutters, and most never open.

Connect AI after that week and you have real things to read. It tunes to your notes, your research, your judgment, and returns customized answers. Hook an empty vault straight up and it can only return the average answer anyone gets.

## 12. Is the free Obsidian enough, or should I buy membership? State the boundary.

Personal use is completely free. Core editing, bidirectional links, and community plugins are all there, with no feature paywall.

Only two things are paid: official cross device Sync, and Publish that turns your vault into a web page for others. Both are extra services, not locks on the software itself. You write on your own computer, notes are all local md, and you sync with a system cloud drive or Nutstore, spending zero. Beginners basically never need these two.

Whether to buy Sync is clear from one calculation. Standard is 4 dollars a month for one vault at 1 GB, Plus is 8 dollars a month for ten vaults at 10 GB, 8 dollars a month is 96 dollars a year, and education gets forty percent off. That money buys peace of mind: end to end encryption, native on all platforms, near real time, built in version history and conflict resolution, and support troubleshoots so you do not. Skip it and do it yourself. Nutstore free gives 5 GB space and 1 GB upload a month, enough for a small vault, tight when the vault grows. Some abandon it for being expensive, others care that they cannot see where their data sits and where it flows. Both are real reasons.

One more thing to know up front: iOS is the dividing line. Many free plans run happily on desktop and flatline on iPhone. Install the mobile app after you have the basic usage working on desktop.

## 13. Can I hand sensitive content (diary / client) to cloud AI right away?

Do not rush to hand it over. Protect what should be protected first, then talk about how smart it gets.

First, cold water. Non targeted stealing of your notes is not a common threat. Attackers want credentials and want ransomware, that pays far faster. What to watch is the openings you actively create. Community plugins have complete and unrestricted access to every file in your vault, and any one plugin can technically read all your notes and attachments and send them out over the network. The official rule for itself is less is safer, redoing even canvas from scratch though an open source library exists, and never running install scripts. Pick plugins in the same posture, install only what you trust.

Do it in two layers. Passwords, API keys, and identity documents should not enter the vault at all, throw them in a password manager. Diary and client material either go through a local model where data never leaves the machine, or get encrypted before cloud, like building a vault with Cryptomator, putting the library inside, with the key only in your hands. Even if the cloud end is breached, what they get is unbreakable gibberish. This path has a side effect: Obsidian cannot read an unlocked encrypted folder, so first time users easily think the data is lost. Remember to unlock the safe before opening the vault.

When you do let AI read your vault, open permissions only to the few folders it needs to finish the job. Do not hand over the whole vault along with unrelated private material.

Resource: Cryptomator https://cryptomator.org/

## 14. Minimum viable path: how do I experience "AI reads my notes" with minimum config once?

The lightest path needs no plugin at all. Go to the Claude web end, create a Project, and upload a few core notes, then every conversation auto links those files.

Zero barrier, good for a first try, with the downside that files update manually and do not sync. To chat inside Obsidian, install the Copilot plugin, enable it after install, and a chat bubble appears in the right sidebar. Then give it a model source, three things: Base URL is the provider address and must end with /v1. Model is the model name, copied exactly. API Key shows only once, store it in a password manager immediately, never into git. After setup type hello in the chat panel, a reply in a few seconds means it works.

If it does not connect, check in this order: did you copy the Key fully, any stray spaces before or after; did the /v1 at the end of Base URL get an extra or missing slash; is the Model name exact; does the account still have balance; if using an overseas model, is the proxy on.

Adjust expectations too. Asking AI what is in notes you wrote yourself has limited value, it is like hiring a helper to read your own writing and paraphrase it back. The truly valuable use is the work you cannot do, like connecting two notes you wrote but forgot, or producing something new from your accumulated pile.

Copy-paste template (use directly):

```
Copilot three settings (follow the plugin's latest README):
- Base URL: provider address, must end with /v1
- Model: model name, copy exactly
- API Key: shows once, store in password manager, never into git

Troubleshooting checklist:
1. Did you copy the API Key fully? Any spaces before or after?
2. Is the /v1 at Base URL end an extra or missing /?
3. Is the Model Name exact?
4. Does the account still have balance?
5. Is the proxy on for an overseas model?
```
