# Chapter 1 · Is FDE a Golden Opportunity, or an Old Job Repackaged?

You have probably been seeing FDE everywhere lately. One post says it is the last mile of AI deployment, another says big companies are fighting over people at million-RMB salaries, another says the role grew dozens of times in two years. It itches to read, but you cannot help wondering: is this thing real, or just another hyped-up concept.

This chapter will not rush to a conclusion for you. Those flashy numbers and titles, we take them apart one by one, to see where the real wave is and where the bubble hides. Read it with your doubt intact. Doubt is not a bad thing. The person who charges in without a shred of doubt is usually the one who gets cut.

---

## 1. FDE hiring grew 42x in two years and 800% in 2025: real wave or a statistics game?

You have surely seen that number, saying these roles grew 42x in two years and kept doubling through 2025. Who would not feel a pull at first glance, yet cool down and wonder: is this tens-of-times growth real demand exploding, or just a tiny base magnified by a multiple?

You have to see what exactly grew. These roles barely existed in 2023; companies could not even write the JD. So rising from near zero to visible makes the multiple look scary. The real question is how many companies today are seriously keeping such people, not just padding the hiring sheet with a fashionable title.

Point the lens at China, and the signal is concrete. ByteDance, Ant Digital, and Zhipu all posted clear roles; Shanghai's Xuhui District even ran the country's first dedicated FDE training class. Overseas is more aggressive: big companies pour billion-scale budgets into dedicated FDE organizations and embed engineers into client companies in batches. But the macro job market is frozen. What is hot about FDE is "the half of the labor market around AI deployment," not a full recovery. Tell that apart and the statistics will not lead you astray.

Yet the other side needs cold water too. Many so-called FDE roles still do pre-sales support and delivery hand-holding; the title changed, the work did not. My read: the demand is real, just amplified by both hype and the multiple. What you should watch is which specific companies are hiring and what they actually do after you join, not just how many times it grew.

## 2. Everyone says FDE is the last mile of AI deployment, but who defines that mile?

The "last mile" phrase is worn out. It sounds right: the model is strong, only the wiring into the business is left. But think closer. Is that mile drawn on a map, or measured on the spot with a ruler?

The truth is the mile has no standard length. In one factory, someone thinks the mile is from demo to launch; another thinks it ends only when front-line workers willingly use it. That is several times apart. Who defines it decides whether FDE is doing technical work or political work.

In the Silicon Valley story, FDE defines that mile itself. It sits in the client's office, watches how people work, writes code by hand to bridge the broken points, and brings the experience back to HQ so the product understands the industry better. That loop closed, the mile becomes asphalt. In China, the definition power often is not in FDE's hands. Shen Yue told of a state-owned firm he rescued: the contract was signed by the big boss, the person executing had no requirements to offer, so FDE had to find, propose, and build everything itself, and ended with seven agents nobody used. The mile turned into a bog because no one stated where the finish line was.

So when you hear "last mile," do not only feel the romance. First find out, in this company, who defines the far end. A business-savvy boss, a hands-off middle layer, or nobody at all. Where the definition power sits basically decides whether you go build a road or go fill a hole.

## 3. How does FDE differ, day to day, from the implementation consultant of ten years ago?

Many old hands see through it at once: this role was called implementation consultant ten years ago, just reskinned with AI. They are half right. The difference is exactly that half.

The implementation consultant's job: the client states the requirements clearly, and the consultant configures the system, trains the users, launches, and leaves. His world is certain; requirements sit in the contract, acceptance criteria in the SOW. When FDE enters, the client often throws one line about wanting AI to cut cost and lift efficiency, unable to say what the problem even looks like. Now FDE must first be a detective and dig out the real problem hidden in the folds of the business.

One example makes the point. A company said it wanted AI customer service. The implementation-consultant playbook would deliver a customer-service system and call it done. But talking deeper revealed the real pain was that several business systems' customer data were completely disconnected; slow service was only the surface. Build only the customer-service system and you get the face, not the root. FDE's value is exactly in going past the surface to move that root.

Technology leverage also splits the two. Ten years ago, implementing an ERP took a team most of a year. Today, one person who can write Prompts and call APIs, with AI coding tools, can stand up a working prototype in two weeks. Work that needed a team now one person covers, and this role's economic value is re-amplified.

So do not rush to call it old wine in a new bottle. The human politics underneath did not change, but the tools smashed the threshold, turning "understands the business AND hands-on delivers" from a luxury into a scalable capability. The consultant delivers a project; FDE delivers a redefined business process.

## 4. Big companies pay million-RMB salaries to grab people: will this role be eaten by standardized products in three years?

Your worry is fair; nobody wants to spend three years training a craft that a product eats next. First see why big companies pay now. Today's enterprise AI deployment has different data, different processes, and different compliance per company; no standard product fits directly. Vendors rush engineers on-site because, in this window, the work can only be done by people customizing.

The question is whether the window closes. Multiple front-line practitioners judge that in two to three years, most industries' deployment playbooks will settle, and companies will return to buying mature products. Then indeed fewer exploration-type FDEs will be needed. From this angle, it is like special forces in a transformation period; once the road is paved, fewer pathfinders are needed.

But do not equate a transitional role with wasted capability. What FDE truly scarcens is sitting at the business site and translating fuzzy needs into a deliverable plan, then banking the experience into something reusable. When standard products spread, the person who understands the business and how to embed tools into an organization becomes more valuable, because they shift from building projects to defining what the product should look like.

So the more practical calculus: do not treat FDE as a title that never changes, treat it as a high-density training period. The industry cognition and client feel you bank in these years cannot be replaced by products. The role may be transitional; the capability persists. The key is whether you used this wind to forge yourself from executor into someone who knows the field.

## 5. I did three years of SWE and switched to FDE: up-leveling or a quiet downgrade?

This question stings because both sides have a case. From a pure coding view, switching to a job with frequent travel, client babysitting, and a lot of dirty work looks like spending your hard-won technical skill on messier tasks. You cannot help doubting: is this a downgrade?

Switch the angle. FDE connects your tech directly to business results. A backend dev after three years often caps at implementing requirements fast and stable; the value sits in the code. FDE's value sits in the business: you personally shove the model into a real pipeline and the savings are real money, closer to the boss and closer to the money. This leap from implementing requirements to defining what should be done is closer to up-leveling.

A ratio tells the story. One practitioner summarized FDE as six parts communication, four parts tech. If your tech base is solid, what you add by switching is communication and industry cognition, exactly the block mid-senior engineers most lack. The stronger some code, the more they get trapped behind the screen, drifting from business truth. FDE forces you out and actually widens your career radius.

Of course, crashing is possible. If you enter and find the work is pure outsourced delivery with zero technical growth, that is a downgrade. So before switching, see whether this company raises FDE as a value deliverer or uses it as a senior implementer. Right direction is up-leveling; wrong direction is the downgrade.

## 6. 95% of enterprise AI pilots produced no return: can FDE really fill that hole?

That number is scary: it says 95% of generative-AI pilots in enterprises produced no business return you can put on a ledger. Naturally you wonder if FDE is the lifeline to fill that hole.

First pull it apart: why is the hole so big. Studying 140-plus failure cases found only a bit over 20% were model, data, or integration problems; the remaining near 80% were stuck in strategy, governance, and change management. That is, the model is not the bottleneck; shoving the model into daily operation is. That is exactly what FDE was designed to do. It does not pick your model; it helps you use the model inside the organization.

But set expectations right. What FDE fills is the deployment link. It sits in the client team, follows from problem discovery to live usage, and closes the two-way knowledge gap. But if a company's boss only wants to buy a gimmick, the middle layer will not cooperate, and the data foundation is a mess, one FDE cannot fill the whole organization's hole.

So the answer has two layers. For the individual, FDE is indeed the most fitting hole-filling role right now; the demand grows right on that point. For the boss, do not expect hiring one FDE to turn stone into gold. It can push a workable project through, but cannot rescue a project doomed to fail. Whether the hole fills depends half on FDE and half on whether the organization itself wants to change.

## 7. My feed is full of "zero background, three months, million-RMB salary": can I trust that course?

Seeing that ad, take a deep breath first. When a role just got hot, the first harvesters are never the people doing the role; they are the people selling the course.

Take it apart and see why it does not stand. FDE depends heavily on industry experience; logistics deployment methods do not transfer to racing at all, and manufacturing experience has no value moved to finance. There is no generic course that can teach everyone. Practitioners on the front line understand this, so some would rather run a few-thousand-person community as a non-profit salon than touch paid training, because industry cognition cannot be rushed; it can only be soaked up on-site bit by bit.

Look at salary. Big companies do pay top talent high. ByteDance's related roles can reach an RMB 1.05 million package; Zhipu's lead role is higher. But that is the tip of the pyramid, not the industry average. Most practitioners, junior tier, earn RMB 20K–30K a month; mid-senior packages are RMB 400K plus; the million-RMB salary concentrates in a tiny few core talents. Promoting the tip as the norm is classic survivor bias.

So can you trust that course. The answer is flat: no. It sells your fear of missing the wave. To really enter, the path is to connect your existing industry background and tech base, and grind in real projects, not pay for a three-month instant-illumination hallucination. The wave is real, but inside the wave the scythes that cut leeks are densest.

## 8. FDE is hot in Silicon Valley: do Chinese companies genuinely lack such people, or just slap on labels?

Bandwagon label-slapping does exist; I will not dodge that. Many domestic roles carry the FDE name but still do pre-sales support or delivery hand-holding; new title, old work. Cloud vendors' agents also love to wear this shell to bid; at the same table fighting for the order are still the same digital-transformation companies.

But the signal of genuine shortage is also hard. ByteDance, Ant Digital, and Zhipu are clearly hiring, and hiring people who can enter the client site. Shanghai's Xuhui District ran the country's first dedicated FDE training class, mixing founders, SOE leaders, and engineers; the government stepping in means it is already treated as an industrial capability to cultivate, not pure trend-riding.

More key is the nature of the gap. Chinese enterprises broadly have a thin digitalization foundation: ERP data inaccurate, MES unused, equipment data scattered. Without these prerequisites filled, no matter how strong AI is, it cannot land. This two-track talent that understands business AND hands-on is inherently scarce domestically; what big companies and agents fight over is exactly this group.

So do not judge in black and white. Some ride the trend, some genuinely lack. The further you go into the sink market, the more you see SMEs the big companies' service cannot cover; they have budget and need but cannot hire a big team, and can only wait for independent service providers to fill. You only need to tell whether the company in front of you is seriously building capability or just wants a fashionable label.

## 9. I am a PM, not an engineer: is there simply no place for me in FDE?

Straight answer: PMs not only have a place, they are the scarce kind in FDE. Many mistakenly think FDE is just a code-writing on-site engineer; that understanding is too narrow. FDE's core is in Forward, stepping forward to the business front line, not waiting in the back for requirements. On this point, a PM's signature skills actually pay off.

PMs already translate requirements, align stakeholders, and turn fuzzy ideas into roadmaps; those are exactly FDE's daily life. One practitioner describes FDE as a consulting advisor who can hands-on deliver: diagnose the problem and also build the solution by hand. PMs are strong on the front half; the weak spot is usually that hands-on build.

Good news: the technical threshold of that back half is lowered by AI coding tools. A PM who understands the business, with the ability to write Prompts and call APIs, can fully build a working prototype alone, without writing systems from scratch like a traditional engineer. A company ran a bootcamp where an art teacher and a finance-role woman with zero background fumbled out an Agent adopted by the company; one was even hired by Geely to lead an AI direction. This shows the on-site hands-on work can be covered by PM-background people too.

So do not be talked out of it by the word "engineer." What you add is the technical feel of turning an idea into something runnable, and the mindset of willing to plunge into the client site. The place has always been there; it is whether you dare walk from the meeting room to the business front line.

## 10. OpenAI building its own Deployment business: real trend or big-company bandwagon?

OpenAI's move to set up an independent deployment company carries weight; it is not a small bandwagon gesture. It invested over USD 4 billion, pulled in 19 ecosystem partners, and acquired a London AI consulting company, taking roughly 150 professional deployment people at once. The same week, another top model company formed a USD 1.5 billion joint venture with a top private equity and an investment bank; the two poured a combined USD 5 billion-plus in the same direction.

Why would a model company not focus on models but send people into client offices. Because the battlefield shifted. The marginal return of the model-capability race is declining; the real second curve is who turns the model into business value in the client's pocket. For every RMB 1 a company spends on software, it averages another RMB 6 on people to land it. This 6-to-1 service market is bigger and stickier than the software itself.

The two play differently, one capital-heavy full ownership, one capital-light joint venture leveraging others, but the underlying judgment is identical: AI's landing point moved from the lab to the client's desk. When these companies entered in person, the consulting-and-deployment giants' stock price dropped a few points that day; the market voted with its feet.

So this is not bandwagon-following; it is the top players placing bets with real money. For the individual, the signal is clear: in the coming years the scarcest are not the people who can train models, but the people who connect models to real business. The big companies entering in person stamped this direction with approval.

## 11. At 35, coding starts to scare me: is FDE an outlet for my age?

The 35-year-old coding panic is not just you; it is the shared situation of many under the big-company cost logic. The window FDE opens for such people is exactly a different one.

Traditional R&D roles compare energy and sprint speed on new frameworks; age makes you passive. FDE reverses and feeds on experience; it wants you to know code, know business, and be willing to run to the client site. A backend dev of eight years understands system complexity and human-game dynamics in ways a fresh graduate cannot catch up to. One practitioner compares it to the product-manager trajectory ten years ago: hot for a while then cooling, but spawning a batch of super-individuals.

More practical is the window period. The next year or two is seen as the key window for traditional enterprises' AI transformation. Building an enterprise system used to take one or two years and millions; now with AI coding you deliver a custom system in two months. In this period, the person who knows the field and has tech is most wanted, because what enterprises lack is exactly someone who can sew AI and business together.

But be clear: FDE is not a shelter for 35; it asks you to leave the comfort zone. You must travel, deal with people, and shift from writing code to defining problems. If you only want to sit safely behind the screen, it may not fit. But if you are worried your experience has nowhere to cash out, it is likely the card that turns years of accumulation into a new position.

## 12. A friend did FDE for half a year and says it is just high-end outsourcing: should I still enter?

Your friend is not entirely wrong, but what he described is a failed FDE, not the whole.

It sounds like outsourcing often because he entered a company that uses him as labor. Front-end sales over-promised a pile to close the deal; he enters to fill the hole and clean up, doing dirty and tired work, far from the boss and far from decisions, so naturally he feels like high-end outsourcing. In that state, his judgment is honest.

But the fundamental difference between FDE and outsourcing is whether he is responsible for finding the real problem. Pure outsourcing is accountable only for the client-defined deliverable; the requirement is ready-made. When FDE enters, the requirement is often fuzzy; he must first penetrate the surface, find the real problem worth solving, then build it himself. If a company only lets you build to spec, that truly is no different from outsourcing. What you should guard against is that company, not the role.

One more layer: many domestic FDEs are carried by cloud-vendor agents, with several layers in between, so projects are easily deformed from the start. Shen Yue flatly says his work is often like a party-B outsourced labor. But among the same group, some plunge into real business, extract implicit rules one by one and codify them into the system, and build things the client cannot leave.

So listen to your friend, do not take it as verdict. If you enter, first see whether you go fill a hole or build a road. Same FDE: some are used as outsourcing, some grow into partners the client cannot leave. The difference is whether the platform lets you touch real problems.

## 13. As models get stronger, will FDE eventually be done in by the models themselves?

This worry is natural; after all, much of FDE's value is in connecting the model to business, and if the model becomes omnipotent, is the middle person still needed.

First separate what stronger models replace. They replace the execution layer: writing code, calling interfaces, building basic architecture. FDE's hard work sits exactly above that layer. A boss shouting "help me cut 3,000 people": the model will not run to align with the business side on its own, will not figure out who in the organization decides, will not push the project through amid employees' fear-resistance. These things the model cannot replace short-term.

Look at the trend. One judgment holds that once everyone who understands the business can use AI tools to deploy themselves, the dedicated FDE role has fulfilled its mission. That has a point, but the premise is everyone who understands business can use it. The reality is most enterprises' organizational maturity is still low; they have not even built the digitalization foundation, and no matter how strong AI is, it cannot land. That gap is FDE's long-term field of play.

Switch to an optimistic angle. The stronger the model, the bigger FDE's leverage. In the past one FDE supported five projects a year; now with AI coding tools he supports ten or more, freeing energy for finding real problems and driving adoption. The model eats repetitive labor and amplifies human judgment.

So do not fear being done in by the model; fear only doing the work the model can do. Root yourself in business understanding and organizational drive, and the stronger the model, the more valuable you are.

## 14. Exactly what kind of person gets amplified doing FDE, and what kind gets slowly ground down?

This question hits the root. Same title, some grow more valuable, some are drained in half a year; the difference is not tech, it is trait.

First, the amplified kind. They enjoy ambiguity. A client throws a vague "want to use AI for efficiency"; they do not panic, they get excited, because it means they get to define the problem. They have a drive that pushes themselves forward with no one giving clear instructions. They also digest the business, willing to soak at the client site watching how people work, extracting implicit rules bit by bit. This kind, after every project, the industry cognition thickens a layer, and the compound grows.

The ground-down are usually the other pole. Strong tech but only willing to stay behind the screen, resisting the moment they hear frequent travel, client babysitting, dirty work. Or they execute FDE as pure outsourcing, doing whatever the demand side says, never asking whether this is really the problem; after a while they find themselves a high-end construction crew with zero growth. Another kind cannot withstand the organization's soft resistance: in SOEs nobody owns the decision, in private firms the boss only asks how many people can be cut; push fails, internal friction consumes them, and they slowly grind themselves away.

One line: FDE amplifies those who can create order in chaos and are willing to plunge into the site, and grinds down those who only want to walk a set process and refuse to touch people and organizations. Tech gets you in the door; trait decides how far you go.
