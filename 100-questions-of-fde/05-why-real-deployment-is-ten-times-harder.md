# Chapter 5 · Why Real Deployment Is Ten Times Harder Than You Imagine

Recruiting posters write "the last mile of AI deployment," annual salary a million, posts surging. But the people actually sent into the enterprise site tell a completely different story. On-site firefighting, demos called unconvincing, a professional team's three-month build beaten by a sixty-something principal's three-day hand-built version. Behind the glossy script, what jams deployment is never how smart the model is; it is the tangled ball of people and affairs no one can untangle. This chapter lays those wrinkles open and sees what FDE actually wrestles with in the real world.

---

## 61. The client says "use AI to boost efficiency"; where does the real pain hide, and how do I dig it out?

The client says use AI for efficiency, but when you really ask, he often cannot say which efficiency to boost. Pain rarely hangs on the lips; it hides in the daily repeated manual work, in the mess of some post nobody wants to take. Digging cannot rely on questionnaires; it needs soaking on-site. One handy method is three layers of questioning: who does this now, how many times a day, what if it goes wrong. Answer these three and the real pain surfaces. There is a more hidden layer: the surface wants a knowledge-base Q&A, but the real problem underneath is often the old expert near retirement, experience that cannot leave. So do not stop at surface needs; ask why a few more times. The hardest part is you often cannot meet the real system users; a layer of requirement-relaying team sits in between, so what you dig is naturally secondhand. Find the people first, then talk plan; reverse the order and everything after is wasted. This also explains why many projects idle on launch day: because from day one they aimed at the wrong problem. Digging is FDE's basic skill; what you train is turning a vague "build some AI" into a concrete pain.

## 62. The requester is the middle digitalization team, not the front-line principal; what if information distorts?

The requester is the middle digitalization team, not the front-line principal who really uses the system; this is too common in deployment. You think you are building for the user, but the whole conversation is with a group carrying digitalization KPIs; by the time they relay front-line appeals a filter has already passed. Deviation starts here, and every later feature may be a guess. The only fix is to bypass the middle layer and reach the front line directly. Even just interviewing two or three real users gives signals solider than ten mid-level meetings. If you truly cannot meet them, list the requirement assumptions one by one and have the middle layer confirm line by line whether this is what the front line really wants, not what he thinks it wants. Do not treat the relay station as the source of needs; this is the pit newcomers most step into. Distorted needs put all later effort on sand; the earlier you correct, the lower the cost. Discover after the system is built that you aimed at a fake need, and that is the real tragedy. So at every requirement review, assume the middle layer misunderstood, verify with that preset, and the distortion rate naturally drops.

## 63. A state-owned project jams on "nobody wants to decide"; how does FDE push without carrying the blame?

What wears a state-owned project down is never tech; it is nobody willing to decide. The contract is signed by the big boss, but landing is thrown downward; the middle layer fears mistakes and will not give clear instructions, so you buzz like a headless fly lucky-dipping across departments. In such a setup, FDE must never carry the blame for others. You cannot push the decision, you can only push the process. One pragmatic move: help the contact build the logic for upward reporting, give him material to hand in, instead of jumping up to decide for the boss. Meanwhile list the small things pushable this week; advance an inch wherever you can. Unclear rights and duties are the client's own disease; you cannot operate, you can only push the fuzzy zone forward inch by inch, and draw a clear line: what you deliver, what the other side must decide. Overstep and take the duty, and when the project dies the blame is all yours. Learning to translate progress into language the other side can report upward is itself a core capability. Many state-owned projects die of drag precisely because FDE could not help replacing others' decisions, ending with chaos in rights and duties, blame still yours.

## 64. Sales over-promised to close the deal; is FDE doomed to fill the hole on entry?

Front-line sales, to sell the product, often call AI omnipotent. By the time you enter, you face the expectation drawn from the promise, an expectation that should never have existed. Filling holes is FDE's norm; the dirty and heavy work mostly comes from here. Silicon Valley FDEs can tell the client straight that this will not work; the domestic chain is long, with agents in between, and what you receive is already an amplified need. The response is not hard confrontation; first break the expectation into verifiable small goals, and every time you hit one, speak with data, slowly chatting the sky back to the ground. Do not expect one talk to reverse it; rebuild trust with continuous small deliveries. You should also crowd forward as early as the pre-sales stage, hear the client's original words earlier, and fill far fewer holes later. You cannot change the promise sales made, but you decide how to fill the hole into steps after entry. The worst is knowing the hole cannot be filled yet still holding on; better lay the risk open early and let both sides face reality. Translating the pie sales drew into executable milestones is the first thing to do on entry; do not wait for the client to smash you with the pie.

## 65. Employees, fearing AI replacement, deliberately trip you with hard problems; how do I break this?

Employees fear AI taking their rice bowl; they cooperate on the surface, trip you with hard problems in the dark; such setups are not rare. Do not dissolve the fear and whatever you push he sabotages. The key to breaking it is not posing AI as the post's enemy. AI now replaces specific tasks, not the whole post; spell this through and the hostility drops a notch. Further, use visible numbers to turn fear into cooperation, say the original three days of work now outputs in half an hour, and the saved time he spends on more valuable things. Let the beneficiary speak for you; more useful than a hundred explanations of your own. Also do not touch his core work at once; first pick his most annoying repetitive labor, let him taste the sweet first. People do not oppose tools; they oppose being quietly replaced by tools. Make the position clear and resistance shrinks. The truly hard part is often not tech integration but the human knot; on this FDE must be more sensitive than an engineer. Once resistance shrinks, he may even proactively help you raise needs; only then do you truly touch the site's real pain.

## 66. I spent three months on full-chain AI; why does it lose to the old principal's three-day hand-build?

A professional team spent hundreds of thousands and three months on full-chain AI, yet lost to a sixty-something principal's three-day hand-built simplified version. Painful to hear, yet a true miniature of deployment. The team chased comprehensive, automatic, pretty; the principal chased something others would use. His version only generates templates, the rest still filled by hand, but other principals love using it because it does not force them to change habits. The professional plan lost by trying to remodel others' work style in one breath, and habits cannot be remodeled. So do not worship full-chain; first ensure what you build sits handy for the other side. The complexity you pride yourself on may be a threshold in the user's eyes. The measure of success was never how advanced the tech is, but whether that person is willing to open it again tomorrow. This slap wakes a kind of arrogance: assuming you know his work better than he does, then reality teaches you a lesson. So before entry ask yourself: is what I build something I want to remodel him with, or something he will handily catch.

## 67. How does over-design happen, and how to judge where the "good enough" boundary lies?

How does over-design come? You want to do it right in one shot, automate everything automatable, connect every connectable link, and end with a spectacular system nobody uses. The boundary of good enough is actually one line: the user can use it without changing original habits; that is good enough. To use your system he must first reshape his workflow; that is over-design. The principal only does template generation, everything else as before; that is partial intelligence, and it lands. Conversely, you insist on digitizing recording, filling, uploading all at once, equal to forcing a rebirth, and it must fail. So before acting ask: can this version let him handily catch it in his original rhythm. If yes, ship; if no, cut. FDE's most valuable judgment often shows in not doing, not in doing much. Few features yet usable far beats full features yet idle; this discipline deserves a spot on the desk. Every extra feature you add is one more reason to leave you hand the user. At review someone thinks features too few; you must explain why few; that is professionalism, not laziness.

## 68. The client's core system has too high switching cost; force it or detour to a plugin first?

The client's core system has run ten years, data tangled like roots; you come in wanting to move the whole process into AI, that is forcing it, and nine of ten collapse. When switching cost is too high, the right play is detour. First build a lightweight plugin or connector around the core system, do not touch its trunk, let AI help from the side, say auto-organize output, generate drafts, do checks. Talk deeper integration after this value is recognized. The cost of forcing: even one misaligned field and the whole project loses trust. The principal's partial-intelligence trick is the same idea; no full refactor demanded, only the most painful point solved. The criterion is simple: does the change force the user off the familiar system. Force, then slow; not force, then push. Do not be led by the client's one line "we want full intelligence"; full often dies in details. You sell a solution not a disruption; the client wants to still open normally tomorrow. The client wants full, you want rhythm; the tug between the two is FDE's daily homework.

## 69. The buyer shouts "AI replaces 3000 people," but AI now replaces tasks not posts; how to calibrate?

The boss opens with wanting AI to replace 3000 people; you must calibrate this on the spot, or the project carries unpayable debt from day one. AI now can replace tasks, not posts. Reviewing contracts this task it can take, but joining negotiations, organizing archives, handling emergencies, those are still human work. Only when over eighty percent of a post's core work is taken over can we call it real replacement; now we are far from that. So do not follow the boss's layoff narrative; pull the talk back to value: after these seven agents go live, how much quality rises, how much more revenue the sales end gains. Replace headcount with verifiable business results and the expectation lands. You also lighten your own load; after all, promising to cut 3000 and delivering seven efficiency tools are completely different holes. The boss's anxiety is understandable, but anxiety cannot serve as a need. Pulling the boss from the layoff imagination back to operating numbers is your first required class on entry. Calibrating is not pouring cold water; it is helping the boss put expectation into a deliverable frame, responsible to both sides.

## 70. Private firms want "get things done," state-owned want "get people handled"; how to switch the two plays?

The two plays must be told apart. The private boss cares about one thing only: spending millions, did you help me grow revenue or cut cost; whether it is AI he does not care, just charge at value. The state-owned setup is completely different; the rights-and-duties game in the organization is ten times more complex than the tech plan, fear of mistakes is the base color, you must first get people handled. Specifically, private firms go straight to the decider, speak with results; state-owned first sort who is responsible, who takes blame, learn to translate progress into language the other side reports upward, do not carry duty for others. One rule works for both: best to meet the person who signed the contract; the closer to the source, the truer the need. The same script cannot break two soils; see which soil this is before sowing. Wrong soil with wrong force, even the best plan grows no sprout; too many have paid this tuition. Wrong direction is the most regrettable, because effort in the wrong soil is just spinning; ten minutes judging soil before entry beats three months of remediation later. Private sees results, state-owned sees people; the two paths' clearance logic differs completely, mix them and you jam.

## 71. On-site travel and accompaniment travel not covered; how does an independent FDE price to not work for free?

On-site, accompaniment, round-trip travel; many small FDEs never count these into the quote, and only when the project runs find income cannot cover the running. A peer flew Beijing to Guangdong four times; travel exceeded project profit, equal to losing-money accompaniment. The first thing in pricing is to list travel separately, either reimbursed or lumped into the total; never vaguely stuff it into labor hours. A steadier posture is to cut residency, switch to remote plus periodic on-site, compress the site to key nodes. Independent takers especially must calculate this account; you sell time and expertise, not free travel. Before quoting ask: who pays this trip's cost, how much, can this one order digest it. If you cannot calculate clear, the more you take the more you lose. Many FDEs fall not on ability but on not counting foot-miles into cost, turning projects into charity. Leaving travel out of the quote model is actively digging your own hole; independent FDEs' downfall often has nothing to do with ability, everything to do with accounting.

## 72. Pay-by-result versus monthly maintenance; which model is steadier and more sustainable for small FDEs?

For small FDEs, pay-by-result sounds tempting but actually presses all risk onto yourself. Result is hard to attribute, the cycle long; the client happy today flips tomorrow, you get no money and nowhere to argue. Monthly maintenance or monthly subscription is far steadier; cash flow predictable, bound to the client as a long-term relation, not a one-shot deal. Of course the monthly model demands you keep producing value, cannot vanish after delivery. The compromise: first do basic accompaniment monthly, talk result-sharing after clear results show, do not bet the whole outcome at once. The mistake newcomers most make is using pay-by-result as a selling point, then the project dies and they worked free. Steadier: surviving beats proving you are great. Sustainable business is a thin stream flowing long, not one all-in; especially when you have no pricing power yet. The monthly model also forces you to deliver visible progress each month, which protects you instead. Wrong model and the project is half-lost before it starts; harder to recover than a tech slip.

## 73. Client data is disconnected, systems old; should FDE fix data first or ship a demo first?

The client's data scatters across a dozen old systems, mutually disconnected; fix data first or demo first, this is a real question. Real deployment has order: first inventory materials, pull out one by one the things scattered in Excel, WeChat groups, old masters' heads; then sort process, set standards; then handle people, because employees fearing replacement will deliberately not cooperate; only then run the minimal validation and build the Agent. Reverse the order and everything before is wasted. What enterprises most lack is often not AI, but the dirty work before AI. So first use a minimal demo to light value and swap for budget, while listing data connectivity as a hard prerequisite in the plan, no hiding. The demo is a match, data is firewood; a match alone cannot light a winter's warmth; those who weight demo and slight foundation all crash at the data gate. AI demands extremely high organizational maturity; the data gate cannot be bypassed; facing it early beats blowing up late. Too many projects think a running demo means all is well, then touching real data find the source is dirty.

## 74. How to explain ROI to a tech-illiterate boss so he keeps investing?

Facing a tech-illiterate boss, the worst in explaining ROI is piling parameters. He cannot hear how many points accuracy rose; he only cares originally two weeks now three days, originally five people now two. Learn to translate tech results into a business account; this is an underrated core FDE capability. The concrete move is speaking with before-after numbers, say the research report shrank from two weeks to three days, lay out the saved time, the fewer human errors, the faster decisions. At worst build a demo with a set of before-after usage data, show the change. The boss wants confidence, not principles. The more you can tell value as an account he understands, the more you get the next investment. Projects that cannot explain ROI, however good the tech, get cut at renewal. Those who can code are many; those who can tell code into an account the boss will sign are the scarce ones. This lesson no one teaches; it is all learned by falling in report after report; fall enough and you naturally know what the boss wants to hear. Translating tech language into the boss's ledger is the watershed that separates FDE from pure engineers.

## 75. Why "system launched does not equal business adopted"; getting the client to truly use it is harder than launch?

The day the system launches you think you won; actually the hardest gate just begins: getting the client to truly use it. Many projects deliver full-featured, yet after rollout nobody touches them, because forcing habit change. Say requiring principals to digitize from recording, filling, to uploading; most find it troublesome, incomplete input gives rotten output, vicious cycle. The root of hard adoption is user habit, not feature count. The fix looks back at the old principal's idea: do not force a rebirth, only slip a handy little helper into the original rhythm. FDE must treat launch as start not end, leave an accompaniment period, watch usage rate not delivery rate. A system running unused equals not built; too many have paid for this with failure. The pen that signs acceptance signs delivery not adoption; do not be fooled by that one page. True delivery happens the countless times the user voluntarily opens the system, not the day the launch announcement goes out. So the accompaniment period is not a freebie; it is part of delivery; cut it and the project mostly will not survive the trial.

## 76. A nine-year veteran says "audit is the whole game"; how exactly should discovery phase investigate?

A veteran says audit is the whole game; those who have stayed on-site understand. Audit sounds scary; someone renamed it sprint and the client cooperates instead. So-called audit is not checking accounts; it is clarifying three things: who exactly is the requester, does the built thing have business value, who owns the rights and duties. Many paralyzed projects show on one check: no clear requester, let alone value, naturally cannot accept. Discovery phase must investigate this way; do not rush to code, and after checking do not rush to model either; step by audit, eval, launch: first use eval to prove Agent behavior controllable, route risk cases to human, then monitor KPIs to earn client trust. Week one on entry do not fantasize solving all problems; first list what can advance, feel out the fuzzy zone inch by inch. Audit's job is fishing that main thread out of chaos; without that thread, the more you write later the more wrong. Laziness in discovery is all repaid at delivery. Many FDEs code on entry and skip audit, only going back to patch when acceptance jams, cost multiplied. One more week in discovery saves one less month of grind later; this account pays any way.

## 77. Architecture before AI, but the client urges an Agent now; how do I hold firm without building messy?

The client urges you to ship an Agent tomorrow; you need a steadying needle inside: architecture before AI. Without first drawing data flow, permission boundaries, human-approval points clear, then piling features on top, it will collapse sooner or later. The way to hold is not hard refusal; first give the minimal architecture boundary: where data comes from, who can touch, which actions must be human-confirmed. Draw the boundary then put the Agent on, even if first only the narrowest scenario. The client wants to see something; you give a stable small thing, more convincing than ten half-products. The temptation of messy building is speed; the cost is every later feature dragging the others. Remember the old principal's trick: partial intelligence beats big-and-complete. The more urgent the urge, the more you must nail the foundation first; this is the line between professional and makeshift. Hold one impulse and you save one rebuild; what the client thanks at last is steadiness not speed. Urge as he may, the foundation is yours to nail; steady or not you know better than him; this composure is the source of professional worth. Holding is not stubborn; it is knowing how fast it collapses; that is called a confident slow.

## 78. 80% written by AI, 20% reviewed by human, 0% AI touches safety payment; how to hold this line?

This human-machine division line deserves welding into the process: eighty percent of content written by AI, twenty percent reviewed by human, zero touch on safety payment. The underlying logic is AI now replaces tasks not posts; drafting, first drafts, these tasks it is faster than human, but key judgment and signing must stay human. For irreversible acts like safety payment, never let AI decide alone; fund transfers, compliance confirmation, these links lock human approval; AI only suggests, never decides. The way to hold the line is removing fund-class and irreversible acts from the Agent's tool permissions, releasing only after human confirmation. The twenty percent human review is not a formality; it catches the one percent deviation that goes wrong. Lose this line and the saved efficiency cannot cover one accident. However fast AI writes, the hand touching money and compliance must be human's own; this is a bottom line not an option. Writing this line into the delivery contract binds more than writing it into internal specs; when something goes wrong there is evidence.

## 79. How to tell whether an AI deployment project is a "tech failure" or a "change failure," and where should FDE exert force?

The criterion for tech versus change failure is clear: the model itself cannot run, the system keeps erroring, that is tech failure; the tech clearly works yet nobody adopts, rights and duties unclear, launch equals not launched, that is change failure. In reality the latter dominates; mass project retrospectives point to one conclusion: among root causes only one is truly technical, the rest are unclear business goals, inadequate organizational change, rotten data governance, no operations plan. So FDE's force should press on the change side: align the boss's expectation, drive real adoption, sort rights and duties. Tech failure you patch engineering; change failure you patch people and organization. Use force in the wrong place and even the strongest model cannot save a company unwilling to change. Recognize this is a human war, not a machine war. Too many hold a hammer looking for nails, yet what jams the project was never that hammer. If your plan only exerts on engineering while no one drives the organization side, failure is near certain, unrelated to model strength.
