# Chapter 4 · How Do I Transition Into FDE?

You have probably already thought about switching. You scrolled job posts for a while, and the more you read the more torn: is my background even qualified, where do I start, should I take a pay cut first. This chapter pours no chicken soup; it helps you pin down the path from each starting point. Backend, product, consulting, data, algorithm, each has its own shortcut and hidden pit. You can keep doubting whether these paths are just another sales pitch; first see clearly, then decide which to trust.

---

## 46. Five years in backend, switching to FDE: build business sense or communication?

Technically you already qualify; what blocks you in FDE is not coding, it is not understanding people. The client talks business and you cannot catch it; once requirements go fuzzy you can only wait for clear instructions, and the job stalls. So do not agonize which to build; business cognition and communication are two sides of one thing, and they grow together on the client site.

The shortest path is not a class, it is the site. Even just following sales to one client, watch how they turn a fuzzy "build some AI" into concrete needs; watch twice and you get it. Then review: which sentence was a real need, which was emotion, and slowly you can tell apart.

Do not start business sense from books, start from asking. Every client meeting, force yourself one more question: "who does this now, how many times a day." Ask enough and the business feel comes. Your code base is solid; the only gap is the string that translates tech language into human words, and that string pulls tightest on-site. Go back and note each client talk in two lines: what he said, what I understood. A month later, the translation ability is trained.

## 47. PM switching to FDE, which hard skills pay off most to learn first?

A PM's short board is the hands. You know business, you can communicate, but to actually build it yourself you must first grow those hands. Do not bite into machine-learning theory first; that pays off slow. First train the ability to assemble a runnable demo; that pays off most.

Specific path: Python as the base, then touch one or two Agent frameworks; the goal is not mastery, it is independently turning an idea into a demonstrable small system. With a demo, your pitch to clients has a completely different confidence, because behind every sentence you say there is something that runs.

Do not chase completeness at once. Train "can build things" first, more useful than memorizing all algorithms. Many PMs switching to FDE stall at "afraid to code"; but FDE does not need engineer-level depth, it needs hands to get the prototype running. Grow the hands and your prior business accumulation instantly becomes an advantage others cannot catch up to. Do not pick a heavy framework; first use a tool that builds an interface in half an hour to stand up the prototype; get positive feedback first, then you survive the dull stretch before sharing.

## 48. Consultant switching to FDE, is the biggest gap writing code or delivering the whole thing?

The gap is personally building it. Consultants are used to delivering a plan, execution handed to others; FDE must get the prototype running himself. The hardest is not which line of code, it is carrying the full chain from zero to demonstrable, with no one backstopping you in between.

My advice: take a familiar industry scenario, force yourself to build a usable small system with low-code plus AI tools. Do not ask for help, do it all yourself. Once you pass this do-it-yourself gate, consulting experience flips into an advantage, because you already know how to break needs and manage expectations.

Writing code, consultants easily overestimate its difficulty, and easily get scared by it. In fact the code on FDE sites is mostly glue and assembly, not building wheels from the bottom. What you really train is the full control from a one-line need to a running system; code is just a tool in it, not a roadblock. Start by building a small tool for some department in your current company; safer than taking outside gigs, and if it flops colleagues backstop you, so you dare to try freely.

## 49. Data-engineering background switching to FDE, how to fill the industry-cognition gap?

Data engineers' tech base is not bad; what lacks is not knowing how this business makes money. You see the data, but not why the person behind the data has a headache, and that jams you at "what to do" rather than "how to do it." Industry cognition has no crash course; it needs soaking.

The most practical move: lock onto one industry, read its prospectus, watch its upstream and downstream, lurk in its practitioner groups, and get familiar with "what they fret about every day." You already understand data; add the business language and you walk into the client site unafraid, catch the pain points they raise, match the metrics they cite.

Do not be greedy; soaking in three industries at once equals soaking in none. Pick one you have some tie to, say the trade of a client you served before; squat half a year and you no longer sound like an outsider. Industry cognition: two weeks on-site beats two months in class. You know SQL, you can directly pull their business tables and see how data flows; more direct than interviews, data speaks for you.

## 50. Traditional algorithm engineer switching to FDE, how to shift the mind from model to business value?

Algorithm people easily sink into whether the model is good enough. FDE's brain asks whether this is worth doing, how much it saves. When switching, swap the question position: ask less whether accuracy rose, ask more whether this scenario is high-frequency, how many manual workers now, how many times better if done.

The model is a means, the business result is the end. Whether you can translate tech trade-offs into accounts the boss understands, on the client site, decides whether you have switched. Many algorithm engineers open with parameters; the boss listens for input-output; this translation ability must be trained.

Do not throw away your prior accumulation either. Model intuition still pays in FDE, especially in eval and guardrails. Only the priority must adjust: first judge worth doing, then think how to do it pretty with a model. Shift the mind and you go from "the one who tunes the model well" to "the one who uses the model to save people trouble," a completely different position. First read the landing reports of your target industry, see what everyone now uses AI to solve, and map your model ability onto concrete business scenarios.

## 51. Can fresh graduates do FDE directly, or is Palantir the only exception?

Most companies will not let a graduate do FDE directly, because what they want is the experience of getting things done in ambiguity, which school cannot give. You have not seen a client flip, have not carried a requirement change; walking in is a disaster. So ordinary people, do not bet on this shortcut.

Palantir is a rare exception; it directly recruits graduates from top schools as forward-deployed software engineers, relying on a mature internal training mechanism that soaks newcomers in real projects. This has little reference value for ordinary people, because no other company has that mechanism.

A steadier path: first be an AI engineer, join a few delivery projects, then switch to FDE. What you bank in those years is "on-site feel," more useful than any certificate. Entering as a graduate is not totally doorless, the door is just narrow; rather than forcing through, first detour to where you can accumulate real combat. Do relevant research in school, touch delivery in internships, all count as strikes. The key is whether you have truly put something into users' hands.

## 52. The JD asks 3 to 7 years; I just hit 3, am I qualified or not?

Three years sits at the lower edge of the threshold, but do not be scared by the number. The JD says 3 to 7 years because it wants someone who can independently make architecture decisions on the client site, not someone with years of service. If in those 3 years you did projects with direct client contact, and independently carried the chain from need to launch, you are qualified to try.

Those asking 5 to 8 years are top roles; ordinary FDE looks at whether you have proof of landing fuzzy needs. The interviewer asks not "how many years have you worked" but "how do you plan to push this scenario." If you answer well, backed by real cases, the years are just a number.

On the flip side, if your 3 years were all pure backend, never touching the business side, then you are indeed unqualified; the gap is not years, it is on-site experience. First find a chance to add one client project, even internal support counts; with that experience, you apply without flinching. Organize your client-related projects into a case book; show it in interviews, more convincing than years. The work speaks for you; years are just the facade.

## 53. Why do Python, SQL, REST API get named in almost every FDE post?

These three are FDE's breathing, not exam points. Python because you must quickly stand up a prototype and connect models; the client wants something and you must run a shadow of it the same day. No Python and on-site you can only stare and wait for backend scheduling.

SQL because the client's real data lies ninety percent in the database. You cannot query, you cannot touch the real shape of the business, only hear retellings, and retold needs are already distorted. Half of FDE's job is pulling real data out to see the problem; without this you are stuck.

REST API because most of FDE's job is connecting different systems. The client's CRM, ERP, reporting systems are each separate; you must call interfaces to stitch them. These three you reach for daily; miss one and you look weak in front of the client. So every JD writes them, not padding, but life-and-death daily. These three are also high-frequency in interview whiteboard tests; practice them and you stay calm on-site. Many posts' first written round tests SQL and interfaces; unavoidable.

## 54. Where does client-communication experience come from; how do non-consultants bank this lesson?

You need not become a consultant. The cheapest drill: be a free FDE, help a friend's small business, help a family shop, use AI to solve one concrete trouble, force yourself to first ask clear "what exactly do you want" before acting. Bank three or five real scenarios and you no longer lack this lesson.

Remember one routine: before every talk, first think clear six things, what the other wants to achieve, why this matters, how the current process goes, where the system leaks, what constraints, what success looks like. Memorize these six questions, slot them into every conversation, and slowly you learn to dig the real need, not take the surface instruction.

Many think communication is a gift; it is muscle. You run a few more times, get dizzy from a client's detours, get pitted by a need, come back and review where you failed to ask through, and next time you are steadier. Consulting experience is valuable because it throws people into such scenes early to grind; you grind with life's small projects, same effect. After each talk note three lines: what he truly wanted, which sentence I misread, how to ask next time. Three months later your eye for people is different.

## 55. That FDE interview "client scenario simulation," exactly what three things does it test?

The interviewer throws a client scenario and watches three things. One, technical depth: when trouble hits, do you think architecture first or dodge first, can you sort out how to act in chaos. Two, customer empathy: can you hear the unspoken real need, not rush to show off code. Three, delivery drive: under pressure do you still want to build something to show the client.

Miss any of the three and the interview is basically hanging. Pure tech but cannot understand people, the client finds you hard to deal with; pure empathy but cannot build, that is sales not FDE; tech and empathy but no delivery drive, cannot push on-site. The interview tests exactly the three present at once.

Daily prep, do not only brush algorithm problems; practice with real cases. Say the client says data is messy, says the demo crashed, says the need is fuzzy; how do you catch each, how turn it into a plan. Practice enough and the interview scene does not faze you, because that is the daily job. You can also reverse-ask the interviewer who this client is and where landing jams, showing you care about real problems not performing. That attitude itself adds points.

## 56. No industry experience, which industry should I dive into first to scale fastest?

Newcomers picking an industry, pick where money is thick, pain points dense, data rich. Finance is the hottest now; banks, insurers, brokerages are all spending on deployment, needs both standard and willing to pay, you walk in and there is work. Manufacturing is also scaling, especially repetitive links like quality check and scheduling, where the boss's pain is obvious.

Do not pick an industry that sounds cool but nobody pays. FDE scales on repeated scenarios and willingness to pay, not novelty of concept. You go to a place that pays and has alike scenarios; do one and you can copy ten, the snowball rolls fastest.

The method is plain: search job boards for AI deployment, large-model landing, see which industry has more posts and similar JDs; that industry is scaling. Follow the money and the posts, steadier than following interest. Do not quit and charge in; after hours lurk in industry communities to scout part-time, low cost, and once sure go all in with a steady mind. Once you stake a case in one, expanding sideways can wait. Even if you finally pick another industry, the industry judgment you soaked is still a plus for other posts; not wasted.

## 57. The three gates "find industry, set direction, throw yourself in": how to choose the first gate without stepping on air?

The first gate most fears choosing by interest. One criterion only: is this industry's people dying over some repeated thing, and willing to pay to solve it. Both conditions met, good prey; only one met, mostly will not scale.

Search job boards for AI deployment, large-model landing, see which industry has more posts and alike JDs; that industry is scaling. Do not pick what sounds lofty; pick what you understand how it makes money and where it currently hurts. Do not understand the money logic and on-site you cannot catch the need either.

Some pick by network, plunge where a friend works; that works too, at least someone opens the door. The key is do not airdrop; first confirm that industry has real, repeated, paying pain points, then enter. Small-cost trial is steadiest: first take one small gig in that industry, verify the pain is real and you can do it, then consider betting your stake. Step firm on the first gate and the next two have ground. Do not charge where someone says the wind blows; more drown in the wind than fly up. Only what you verified yourself counts.

## 58. Why can industry cognition not be rushed, and in what position should I slowly "soak" it out?

Industry cognition is soaked, not lectured. You can soak in two positions. One is the client site, truly join projects, watch what they argue about daily, where they jam, who decides, who trips; these books do not write. The other is industry communities, lurk in practitioner groups and forums, read their complaints.

The key is not memorizing terms, but understanding what keeps this business turning, what it fears going wrong. Once you know its life gate, the client opens his mouth and you know what he fears, what he wants. This tongue sense, a hundred lectures cannot buy; it can only be slowly pickled in a real environment.

Soak half a year and you no longer sound like an outsider. Do not expect a three-month crash course to give you industry cognition; that only gives you a set of scripts that crack on stage. Spend ten minutes daily reading that industry's vertical media and official accounts, keeping the tongue sense alive. Cognition is not an epiphany one day; it is the accumulation of daily soaking. Stay honestly on-site or in the circle, and time grows the cognition for you.

## 59. I want to be a "grassroots FDE" serving SMEs; where do the first clients actually come from?

A grassroots FDE's first clients basically do not come from ads; they roll out of the acquaintance network plus small-win cases. First look inside your original network, help a friend's company use AI to solve one concrete trouble, finish it, get paid, make it presentable, and this one case brings the next.

SME bosses trust word-of-mouth over resumes. You only need one demonstrable result and referrals move. They do not care which company you came from, only whether you can bite off the troublesome job that aches them. So at the start do not think about volume; first serve one client well, make him willing to recommend you to peers.

Some start from freelance platforms; that is colder to start and fiercer to compete. Steadiest is still cutting in from the trade you know and the people you know, first use low price or even free to swap a pretty case; case in hand, the later pricing power is yours. After finishing, write one page of case, clear on how much saved and how done, easy for clients to forward. Bosses dislike resumes, they like this forwardable proof.

## 60. Switching to FDE, should I take a pay cut first; how big is the cost of cutting from the original post?

Whether to cut pay depends on where you cut from. If you cut from a big-company high-salary pure-tech post to independent FDE, with no clients for the first months, income may indeed drop a notch. Independent starting monthly income often sits between RMB 20K and 40K, thinner than a big-company pure-coder package.

But switching internally to FDE, most do not cut but rise, because you are closer to business and value is visible. Do not count only the monthly salary; count whether you can faster bank the asset of "independent delivery." That thing long-term is worth more than a moment's pay, because it frees you from depending on one post.

Some do both: first sneak FDE work inside the job, then go full-time once cases suffice. This costs least, income never breaks, experience still banks. While employed, first talk one or two private gigs, verify you can really sell before discussing stay or leave. Use the smallest trial cost to swap the steadiest turn. The most feared switch is quitting bare to force a turn, turning economic pressure into anxiety and then performing worse. Many crash by treating the switch as cutting an arm to survive; actually it is more like sideways changing lanes, keep a buffer and you run faster. Steadier: first slice in sideways, then fully switch.
