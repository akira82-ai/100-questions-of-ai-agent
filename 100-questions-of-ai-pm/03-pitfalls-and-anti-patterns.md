# Chapter 3 · Pitfalls and Anti-Patterns: How AI Products Get Killed

## 36. Why does making "daily feedback count" the north star metric make the model output extreme content after 3 months? Where does flywheel-as-KPI go wrong?

Treating the flywheel as a KPI is one of the most hidden and fatal anti-patterns in AI products. The symptom is typical: the team writes "daily feedback count" or "data collection volume" into the OKR and starts chasing the number hard. Three months later, model quality not only did not rise, it started outputting strange content, and user complaints surged.

The root cause is that KPI-ification distorts the whole data-collection mechanism. Pushed by the KPI to deliver data, the team starts finding ways to raise the feedback rate: bombarding users with feedback pop-ups, using all kinds of manipulative copy to get clicks, even making the feedback button more prominent than the answer. The result is severely polluted data: feedback clicked just to close a pop-up, extreme-emotion feedback induced by manipulation, fake feedback mass-produced by bots, all flowing into the training set. The model fine-tuned on this noisy data starts to drift: outputs that were stable become unstable, outputs that were safe start touching content red lines. Treating the flywheel as a short-term KPI sacrifices long-term value.

The right move is to run the flywheel as a long-term asset. The core metric should be data quality: "how much feedback was verified as a valid signal," not "how many feedback entries were collected." For the flywheel to turn long, the speed cannot be too fast. Forcing it to turn only breaks it apart.

## 37. Collecting data without labeling, why will the flywheel never turn?

This is the most common way the flywheel dies. The team launches an AI product, hooks up complete data collection, produces millions of user-behavior records a day, stores them in the data lake, and then nothing. The data lies in the lake, never labeled, never turned into trainable samples, never used to fine-tune the model. The flywheel turns for a year and the model is still the one at launch.

Data itself has no value; what has value is turning user behavior into trainable signals, and the key link in that conversion is labeling. Without labeling, data is just a pile of 0s and 1s the algorithm team cannot use. Collect-only-no-label, the root cause usually ties three things together: nobody owns it, budget not approved, no evaluation set built. The PM thinks labeling is the algorithm team's job; the algorithm team thinks it is the PM's; in the end nobody manages it. Labeling costs money, either hiring annotators or using LLM-as-a-Judge; without budget approval it cannot be done. Even if labeled, without an evaluation set there is no way to verify whether it is useful, and the motivation to label weakens further. The three reasons reinforce each other and the flywheel jams completely.

For the flywheel to turn, labeling must enter the product plan from day one; the PRD stage must define the labeling flow, labeling dimensions, and labeling budget. Collect-only-no-label is like piling raw material in a warehouse and never putting it into production; the warehouse fills but the factory stays idle.

## 38. The flywheel turns only in the algorithm team's hands. What happens when the PM is absent?

The flywheel is a cross-team systems engineering effort. Letting it turn only in the algorithm team's hands with the PM absent is another typical anti-pattern. The symptom is typical: the algorithm team buries its head in model optimization, BLEU up 5 points, ROUGE up 3 points; brought before the business side, the business side is blank: what does this score have to do with my business? The data-collection schema the algorithm team built is all in technical language the PM cannot read and the business side understands even less; the collected data and the metrics the business cares about completely mismatch. The evaluation set the algorithm team built covers only model-capability-level cases; not one of the scenarios the business actually cares about. With the PM absent, the flywheel becomes technical self-indulgence: the more refined the model, the more still the business value.

The PM's duty in the flywheel is translation and alignment. Translate the metrics the business cares about into collectable signals, so the algorithm team knows what to optimize. Translate the algorithm team's results into business language, so the business side knows what value the optimization brought. Make the data, algorithm, and product teams use the same schema, the same evaluation system, the same goal. This is something neither the algorithm team nor the business side can do; only the PM can. If the PM does not join the flywheel, the flywheel loses direction; turn it as fast as you like and it is still spinning empty.

## 39. If the feedback cycle exceeds 30 days, is the flywheel already dead?

For the flywheel's speed, one metric is enough: from the moment the user acts to the moment that action becomes a model improvement, how many days pass in between. This cycle is called the data-to-training cycle; 30 days is the floor, beyond 30 days the flywheel is basically dead.

Why is 30 days the line between life and death? Because the change cycle of the market and user behavior is usually within 30 days. A data point reflects the user preference at the time it was produced; 30 days later the preference may have changed. A model trained on data from 30 days ago, when launched, faces a new batch of users; the data is stale, the model optimizes past problems.

The root cause of a long feedback cycle is usually an engineering-pipeline problem. A week stuck between collection and cleaning, a week between cleaning and the labeling platform, a week between labeling done and model training, another week between training done and gray-release launch; add them up and it is a month. To compress this cycle, the whole chain must be automated: auto-ingest from instrumentation, auto-run cleaning batches, auto-scoring by LLM-as-a-Judge, auto model training, auto gray-release.

The ideal state is to keep the data-to-training cycle within 7 days; 7 days means shipping a fine-tuned version every week, the model and market evolving in sync. If 7 days is impossible, at least hit 14; 30 days is the floor, beyond that is a dead flywheel. The flywheel does not just need to turn; speed decides everything.

## 40. User privacy data goes straight into the training set. When does this pit finally blow up?

This is a time bomb, not a question of whether it blows but when and how badly. The phenomenon is common: to accumulate training data fast, the team pours users' real conversations, real behavior, even real identity info straight into the training set. The short-term temptation is huge: real data has the highest quality, the effect lift is most obvious, and the more the team tastes the win the more it collects.

But the compliance risk of this is extremely high. China's Personal Information Protection Law and Generative AI Service Management Measures have strict rules on user data entering training: the data use must be clearly disclosed to users, user consent must be obtained, and data must be desensitized. Overseas, GDPR is stricter; discovered user privacy data in training means sky-high fines. The way it blows is usually not active regulator investigation, but users discovering it themselves: a user asks the product a question and the model answers with another user's private information. Once that happens it is a PR disaster; the product may be taken down directly.

The right move is to build three lines of defense. The bottom layer is data desensitization: all data entering training, sensitive fields like identity, contact, address, all desensitized. The middle layer is compliance review: before the training set launches, legal or compliance must review it. The top layer is user authorization: the product agreement clearly discloses data use and provides an opt-out. Without these three layers you are running naked; it is only a matter of time before it blows.

## 41. After the Agent's tool count went from 3 to 10, task success rate dropped 40%. Where is the tipping point of tool bloat?

Agent products have a counter-intuitive rule: more tools is not better; past a certain tipping point, more tools make the Agent dumber. The symptom is typical: the team starts by giving the Agent 3 tools, task success rate 85%; the business side feels it is not enough and asks to add tools, up to 10, and the success rate drops to 45%.

The root cause is that more tools means a larger choice space for the Agent, and a larger probability of choosing wrong. The model has to judge which of 10 tools to use; the cognitive load is several times that of 3 tools, the error probability rises, and the whole task chain collapses. Tool bloat also brings another problem: the boundary between tools blurs, the Agent does not know whether to use A or B and starts trying randomly; the cost of trial and error is doubled latency and cost.

The tipping point of tool count relates to task complexity. Simple tasks need 3 to 5 tools; complex tasks at most 8 to 10; beyond 10 you must do tool grouping or tool routing. Tool grouping classifies tools by scenario, exposing only the relevant subset each time, like a customer-service scenario exposing only ticket tools, refund tools, query tools, not code-writing tools. Tool routing adds a routing Agent layer that first judges the task type, then decides which tools to expose to the execution Agent. Both effectively ease tool bloat.

More tools is not more power; an Agent's ceiling depends far more on the design quality of the tools than on their number.

## 42. Users say it is "hit or miss," but your offline Evals are all green. How do you close this gap?

Offline Evals all green, yet user feedback is poor; this is the most common nightmare for an AI PM. The root of the gap is that offline Evals are disconnected from real user scenarios. Offline Evals usually run on a test set carefully picked by the team; these cases are known, standard, clearly bounded. Real user questions are nothing like that: users speak in dialects, mistype, ask completely unforeseen questions, bundle several questions into one. However well offline Evals run, if they do not cover these real scenarios it is self-deception.

Closing this gap takes three directions at once. The most fundamental is to broaden the evaluation set's source: do not use cases the team dreamed up; sample from real user logs and add the questions users actually asked. Next is layered evaluation: smoke tests secure core cases from regressing, regression tests cover routine scenarios, boundary tests probe capability limits, adversarial tests defend against misuse and attacks; all four layers are mandatory. Last is online monitoring: offline Evals only tell you whether the model is OK before release; online monitoring tells you the real user experience after release. The key metrics to instrument online are the user's next action after getting the answer: copied, shared, continued asking means satisfied; closed immediately, regenerated, transferred to human means unsatisfied.

Align these implicit signals with offline Evals and the gap closes. All-green offline Evals do not mean users are satisfied; user satisfaction can only be read from real online behavior.

## 43. After the multi-Agent system launched, latency spiked. Cut Agents or cut features?

After a multi-Agent system launches, the most common failure point is latency. In the single-Agent era latency stayed under 10 seconds; once multi-Agent collaboration comes on, latency often shoots past 30 seconds. Users will not wait 30 seconds; 7 seconds is already the cliff edge of experience, beyond 7 seconds retention drops off a cliff.

When latency spikes, the first judgment the PM must make is whether this latency is necessary or optimizable. Necessary latency is the communication cost multi-Agent collaboration itself brings: Agents must call each other, aggregate results, coordinate decisions; all take time, and if the business truly needs multi-Agent collaboration it must be accepted. Optimizable latency is often an engineering problem: the protocol between Agents is too verbose, each step waits for the previous step to fully finish before starting the next, no parallelization.

After judging, if latency truly cannot be compressed, you must subtract. Subtraction has two directions: cut the Agent count, or cut feature complexity. Cutting Agent count changes unnecessary collaboration to single-Agent handling; three Agents were divided, and one is found to have very little work, so it can be merged into another. Cutting feature complexity breaks the task finer, using multi-Agent only for the sub-tasks that truly need it, deterministic flows for the rest. The principle of cutting is to keep only the core scenarios where multi-Agent is truly much better than single-Agent; for others, fall back to single-Agent or workflow. Latency is the lifeline of user experience; sacrificing latency for multi-Agent is taking the means for the end.

## 44. Why can Prompt Injection not be blocked technically, and how should the PM design product-layer defense?

Prompt Injection is the hardest security hole to defend in AI products. It cannot be blocked technically because how large models work means they cannot distinguish "instruction" from "data." A user inputs a sentence; the model cannot judge whether it is the user's real intent or an attacker's carefully constructed malicious instruction; both look like legitimate natural language. Classic attack methods include making the model ignore the previous system prompt, making the model output its initial instructions, making the model play a role with no limits.

Technical defense failing does not mean the product layer has no options. The PM can design multi-layer defense at the product layer. The outermost is input filtering: use rules or a small model to scan user input first and identify obvious attack patterns; this layer cannot block advanced attacks but blocks most low-level ones. One layer in is output review: after the model generates an answer, pass it through a safety filter that identifies violating content, sensitive info, and over-privileged actions, blocking or rewriting them. Further in is permission isolation: the tools the Agent can call must be designed with least privilege; read-only if possible, do not grant write; scope-limited if possible, do not grant full access. The innermost is human fallback: high-risk operations must be confirmed by a human; no matter how certain the model is to execute an action, anything involving money, data, or outbound sending must have a confirmation step.

Stacking these four layers can bring Prompt Injection risk down to an acceptable range. Blocking it completely is impossible, but you can make the cost of attack far exceed the gain. Security has no silver bullet; multi-layer defense plus human fallback is the only viable path now.

## 45. Why do your RAG retrieval results look "relevant" but users say they are useless?

This is a particularly maddening problem in RAG projects: recall over 90%, the top-3 results look relevant to a human, yet users say they are completely useless. The root cause is that "relevant" and "useful" are two different things. Vector retrieval measures semantic similarity; a text semantically related to the question gets retrieved. But the user does not want semantic relevance; they want the question answered. A text related to the question but containing no answer is useless to the user. The typical scenario: the user asks "what is the return process," the top three retrieved are all background on return policy, none states the specific return steps; each is relevant, each is useless.

Solving this gap takes both retrieval and generation ends. On the retrieval end, add re-ranking: the re-ranking model looks not only at semantic similarity but at whether this text can directly answer the question; with re-ranking, texts that answer the question get pushed to the front. On the generation end, do answer verification: after the model generates an answer, verify it truly comes from the retrieved context and truly answers the user's question; if verification fails, either regenerate or honestly tell the user "I did not find an accurate answer." Another often-ignored point is query rewriting: the user asks "return process" but really means "the specific steps of 7-day no-reason return"; without rewriting, the retrieval direction is wrong.

Relevant is not useful. Thinking this through clearly makes the RAG user experience much better.

## 46. The business side says "the effect is bad." Do you go to the algorithm first, or check product and data quality first?

A business-side "the effect is bad" is the most common feedback an AI PM receives. This sentence carries extremely low information but triggers huge chain reactions. The most common wrong reaction is to immediately go to the algorithm team; they are called in to troubleshoot, spend three days tuning the model, and find the model has no problem at all.

The right move is to first break down what "bad effect" actually means. Bad effect may show in five ways: answering the wrong question, answering incompletely, fabricating content, answering wrong, answering nothing; each has a completely different root cause and fix. After breaking it down, check product and data quality first, then the algorithm. Check data first: are the docs in the knowledge base the latest, do they contradict each other, did parsing go wrong; data-level problems cannot be saved by any amount of algorithm tuning. Then check product: what is the real distribution of user questions, did the product's fallback logic take effect, did the feedback loop collect the bad cases back; product-level problems the algorithm also cannot solve.

Only after both layers are checked and confirmed fine does the algorithm get its turn. Algorithm-level problems usually show as the model's capability ceiling: same data, same product logic, swapping in a stronger model might help a bit, but this lift is usually gradual, not a qualitative change. When the business side says the effect is bad, 80% of the root cause is in product and data, 20% in the algorithm; checking the wrong direction first just wastes time.

## 47. How much does your AI product lose per call? Beyond Token, have you counted these 5 hidden costs?

For the unit economics of an AI product, most teams count only Token cost; this is a severe underestimate. The real per-call cost contains at least six parts.

Everyone counts Token cost; that is the first part. The second is latency cost: every second the user waits is potential churn; higher latency means lower conversion; this part must be folded into the per-call cost. The third is fallback cost: when AI cannot answer it goes to a human, whose cost is far higher than Token; if a customer-service AI's human-takeover rate is 30%, then 30% of call cost must be counted as human. The fourth is iteration cost: the model is fine-tuned every week, the evaluation set updated every week; this R&D labor must be amortized per call. The fifth is data cost: labeling, cleaning, storage; every link of the turning flywheel burns money. The sixth is compliance cost: filing, review, legal consulting; these hidden costs are often ignored.

Adding the six parts, the real per-call cost of many AI products is 3 to 5 times the Token cost. Token cost looks transparent because the model vendor bills it clearly; the other costs look opaque because they are scattered across different teams and budget items. What the PM must do is sum these six costs into one real unit-economics table. Without this accounting, pricing is a guess; guess at pricing and the hotter the product the more you lose.

## 48. The model did not error but silently got worse. How do you spot it before user complaints?

The model vendor pushes a new version; system logs are all normal, no errors, no anomalies; but users start slowly churning and the complaint rate slowly rises. This phenomenon is called silent degradation: the model did not error but the effect worsened.

The root cause is that the model vendor's "version upgrade" is transparent at the API level; the API is still the same API, but the model behind it may have changed. The new model is better on some dimensions and possibly worse on others; the scenarios your product depends on may happen to be the worse part. Finding silent degradation through system logs does not work; it requires business-metric monitoring, and not technical metrics like error rate or latency, but user-behavior metrics: copy rate dropped, share rate dropped, session length shortened, regenerate rate rose, human-transfer rate rose; these are the early signals of the model quietly worsening.

A more proactive approach is to build a regression test set. Prepare 100 to 200 core cases; each time you suspect the model changed, run this set and see if the score dropped. If it dropped, immediately contact the model vendor to confirm the version, and roll back to the old version if necessary. Silent degradation is an AI-product-specific operations problem; a traditional software bug explodes in the logs, an AI product's degradation hides in user behavior. Without proactive monitoring, by the time complaints burst the damage is already done.

## 49. The AI gave a wrong answer. Why did the user not click the "feedback" button?

This is the most hidden reason the flywheel will not turn: the feedback button is right there, but users just do not click it. The feedback rate is usually only 1% to 3%, meaning 97% of the user experience is not collected. Users do not click feedback not because the experience is good, but because the cost of clicking is too high and the payoff too low. The cost is high: stop and think about where the answer was wrong, organize language to write feedback, worry whether anyone will read it; the whole process is a heavy cognitive load. The payoff is low: clicking feedback will not make the model better immediately; next time it is still as bad; with no positive feedback the user naturally stops clicking.

To get users to give feedback, you must lower cost and raise payoff at the same time. Lowering cost means making feedback extremely light: do not make users write text; a dislike, a copy, a regenerate is enough; these implicit signals are more real than explicit feedback. Which part the user copied shows which part was useful; which words the user edited, the larger the edit distance the more dissatisfied; closing the page after a few seconds shows the content had no value. Raising the payoff means letting users see the effect of feedback: next time the user asks a similar question, actively tell them "based on your last feedback, we optimized this scenario," letting the user perceive their feedback was used.

Explicit feedback rate will always be low; putting implicit signals to use is the real fuel of the flywheel.

## 50. Why is 7-day retention only 8% after adding an AI chat box? What do the 4 death paths of AI-feature retention look like?

An AI feature launches, 7-day retention only 8%. The team is confused: the model is clearly strong, the experience clearly stunning, why do users leave after one use? There are four death paths for AI-feature retention, occurring at different times with different root causes.

The fastest is novelty death, at 3 to 7 days. The user is amazed the first time, feels it is just so-so the second, starts to find it boring the third; the root cause is the product provides only novelty, not sustained value, the user comes to play once and leaves. Next is trust-collapse death, at 7 to 14 days: the user found it magical the first few times, then on the fourth hits a low-level mistake by the model and instantly loses trust, never uses it again; the root cause is the model's worst case is too shocking, the user remembers one catastrophic error more firmly than ten successes.

Further is efficiency-inversion death, at 14 to 30 days: the user finds using AI is slower than doing it manually, and still has to verify results, so might as well do it themselves; the root cause is the product did not really save time but added cognitive load. The slowest is value-misalignment death, beyond 30 days: after using it for a while the user finds this AI feature does not solve what they really wanted; the root cause is the product built what the team could build, not what the user wanted.

Corresponding to the four death paths, the fixes are completely different. Novelty death needs sustained capability updates and a discovery mechanism, letting users keep finding new uses. Trust-collapse death needs fallback mechanisms and trust calibration to control the worst case. Efficiency-inversion death needs UX optimization to truly save time. Value-misalignment death needs to return to user research and redefine the product value proposition. Retention is the life-and-death line of AI products; without diagnosing the death mode clearly, you cannot prescribe the right fix.

## 51. Users finish with AI and go back to manual operation. This is not a model problem; which link is missing in your trust design?

Users use AI, then go back to manual operation; this phenomenon is called trust regression, an AI-product-specific retention killer. It is not entirely the same as the model not being strong enough; in many scenarios the model's answer is clearly correct, yet the user still wants human confirmation; the problem is that the user's trust in AI did not match AI's actual capability: either over-trust met one error and fully regressed, or they never dared to trust from the start. Both paths end the same: the AI is left idle.

From the pitfall view, the root cause of trust regression almost always jams at the "error-recovery cost" link. When the user finds the AI wrong, whether they can easily fix, roll back, take over decides whether they dare use it next time. If fixing it once is more troublesome than doing it themselves, the user will bypass the AI next time, preferring to do it themselves from the start. This link is more damaging than capability transparency and confidence visualization, because the first two affect whether the user dares to use it the first time; the error-recovery path affects whether the user dares to use it again after being burned.

Capability opacity certainly pushes too: after using it a few times the user figures out the boundary and immediately regresses when finding something it cannot do. Invisible confidence also wears patience: the user must judge every time whether this output is trustworthy, and after judging a few times feels tired and simply goes to human for everything. But among these links, the error-recovery path is the most easily ignored and the most fatal, because it directly decides the user's choice after "being burned once." Trust design is not a nice-to-have; it is the underlying infrastructure of AI-product retention.

## 52. Can a product with Token at 40% of revenue survive? Where do the 5 categories' cost red lines sit?

The ratio of Token cost to revenue is a key metric for whether an AI product can survive, but the cost red lines differ greatly by category; measuring all products by one standard misjudges.

Tool-type AI products have the tightest red line: users pay to complete a specific task, like writing copy, translating, generating images; once Token cost exceeds 15% to 20% of revenue, the gross margin cannot hold. Subscription AI products are slightly looser: users pay monthly or yearly, unlimited use; the red line is 25% to 30%, because some users use little, pulling the average cost down. Ad-based AI products are the most fragile: users use free and monetize by ads; the red line is only 10% to 15%, because ad ARPU is low, and slightly higher Token cost means a loss.

Enterprise AI products have a much wider red line: customers pay by API call volume or by effect; the red line reaches 30% to 40%, because enterprise customers have strong paying power and can bear higher cost. The widest is Agent-type products: a single task involves multi-turn calls, Token consumption is naturally high; the red line is 35% to 45%, beyond 45% is basically a dead end. Whether a product with Token at 40% of revenue can survive depends on the category: enterprise and Agent types can still hold; tool and ad types are basically dead ends. Judge which category your product is in, compare to the red line, and you know how much optimization room is left.

## 53. Why did your monthly bill triple on the day GPT dropped 75%? How does the Jevons paradox manifest in AI products?

GPT drops in price, yet the user's bill goes up; this looks counter-intuitive but repeats in AI products. There is a phenomenon in economics called the Jevons paradox: when the use efficiency of a resource rises, its total consumption increases. Coal prices fall, more people buy steam engines, total coal consumption rises; oil prices fall, more people buy cars, total oil consumption rises.

The logic is the same in AI products. The unit price of the model falls, the team starts designing more complex products: the Agent takes more steps, RAG retrieves more times, multimodal processes more input types; the unit price fell but call volume exploded, and the total bill rose. In the wave where GPT dropped from 30 dollars to 3 dollars, many teams' monthly bills tripled, not because the team got lavish, but because the product shape changed: multi-Agent systems once too risky to build are now built, multi-turn dialogues once too risky to open are now open, long contexts once too risky to use are now used.

The lesson of the Jevons paradox for the PM is that a price cut is not a money-saving silver bullet. A price cut changes the economic-feasibility boundary of the product, making previously unprofitable features profitable; the team will instinctively explore these new boundaries, and call volume rises. To control total cost, you cannot only watch the unit price; you must watch call volume, product shape, and user usage patterns. The unit price is decided by the vendor; call volume is decided by the product; the real cost-control lever is on the product side.

## 54. Why do AI product A/B tests always reach wrong conclusions? How to redesign experiments for probabilistic output?

The A/B test logic of traditional products is simple: group A uses plan A, group B uses plan B, compare the metric difference, adopt the winner if significant. For AI products, this logic fails.

The failure is because AI output is probabilistic. The same user, the same input, plan A and plan B may give completely different outputs; even if the two plans are equal in expectation, a single A/B test may reach the opposite conclusion. Worse, AI-product metrics naturally have high variance: the same batch of users' satisfaction today and tomorrow may differ a lot, not because the plan changed but because of the model's own randomness. High variance plus probabilistic output makes traditional A/B test sample-size calculation completely invalid; an experiment calculated to need 10k samples may actually need 100k for a credible conclusion.

Redesigning AI-product experiments takes three moves. First, lengthen the experiment period: where a traditional product runs 7 days, an AI product needs at least 14 to 21 days to offset the noise of probabilistic output. Second, increase the sample size: the traditional product's sample-size formula must be multiplied by 5 to 10 times for AI products; insufficient sample size means the conclusion is noise. Third, introduce offline Evals for cross-validation: online A/B testing tells you user behavior changed, offline Evals tells you model output changed; only when the two align can you confirm a real difference from the plan. Without these three moves, AI-product A/B testing is dice-rolling: today it concludes A beats B, redo it tomorrow and it may conclude B beats A.

## 55. Model routing sounds beautiful. Why can most teams not pull it off?

Model routing became a particularly hot concept from 2025: simple tasks route to a small model to save money, complex tasks route to a large model to keep quality. It sounds like the perfect plan, balancing cost and quality, but in reality most teams cannot pull it off.

The first reason they cannot is that routing itself also errs. Routing relies on a small model or rules to judge task complexity, and this judgment itself has error: a simple task may be misjudged as complex, wasting money on a large model; a complex task may be misjudged as simple, and quality collapses. If the routing error rate exceeds 15%, the saved cost is offset by quality loss; beyond 30% model routing is negative optimization. Further, routing adds system complexity and latency: one model call to judge, another to execute, total latency doubles; for latency-sensitive scenarios model routing is basically unusable, users will not wait 10 seconds plus 10. There is a more hidden cost: different models have inconsistent output styles; the same product gives the user a small-model answer this time and a large-model answer next time; the style difference confuses users, and the experience drop from inconsistency is often worth more than the Token cost saved.

Model routing that truly works is for scenarios with clear task boundaries, extremely high cost sensitivity, and low requirements for latency and style consistency, like batch data processing, offline tasks, internal tools. For most ToC products, model routing's gain does not cover its cost; sounds beautiful, but using it is a pit.
